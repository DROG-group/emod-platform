## Purpose

You'll learn why "outrageous" beliefs persist—and why calling them outrageous doesn't help you understand or counter them.

---

## Scenario

A colleague dismisses a belief as "outrageous" and assumes anyone holding it must be stupid or malicious. You need to provide more useful analysis. You have:

- **Time available**: Quick reframe of the analysis
- **Stakes**: Misunderstanding beliefs leads to failed interventions
- **Data access**: The belief and its context
- **Constraint**: Your intuition about what's "outrageous" may not match others'

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What function does this belief serve for believers?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: How does understanding function change intervention?

---

## Method: Belief Function Analysis

### Start here: Suspend the judgment

"Outrageous" is your reaction, not an analysis. Ask instead:
- What problem does this belief solve for believers?
- What psychological or social need does it meet?
- Why is it more attractive than alternatives?

### Step 2: Identify the function

Beliefs can serve:
- **Explanatory**: Makes sense of confusing events
- **Identity**: Marks group membership
- **Agency**: Provides sense of control in chaos
- **Community**: Creates belonging with fellow believers

### Step 3: Design function-aware intervention

If you understand the need, you can:
- Offer alternative that meets same need
- Avoid interventions that threaten identity
- Work with the function, not against it

---

## Why "Outrageous" Beliefs Persist

**They work** (for believers):
- Provide simple explanations for complex events
- Create clear enemies and allies
- Offer community with others who believe
- Give sense of special knowledge or agency

**Dismissal doesn't work**:
- "You're wrong" doesn't address underlying needs
- Mockery strengthens in-group bonds
- Facts don't address identity functions

**The gap**: You're correcting claims. They're maintaining identity.

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong analysis** (belief function understood):
- Specific needs identified
- Intervention addresses function
- Doesn't assume believers are defective

**Medium analysis**:
- Some function identified
- Partial intervention design
- Still some "they're just wrong" framing

**Weak analysis**:
- "They're crazy/stupid/evil"
- No function identified
- Intervention relies on shame or facts alone

---

## Stop Rule

Stop calling beliefs "outrageous" when:
- **It's not analysis**: It's your emotional reaction
- **It prevents understanding**: Can't design intervention without understanding
- **It backfires**: Mockery strengthens the belief

---

## DIM Mapping

Understanding belief function informs intervention choice:

**Gen 2** (Debunking): May fail if belief serves identity function
**Gen 3** (Prebunking): Can inoculate before identity attachment forms
**Gen 5** (Interaction): Design environments where healthier beliefs meet same needs

---

## Key Takeaway

"Outrageous" beliefs serve functions. Understanding those functions is prerequisite to effective intervention. Dismissing believers as stupid or crazy isn't analysis—it's giving up on understanding.

---

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: A conspiracy theory provides believers with: sense of special knowledge, community with other believers, explanation for events that feel random.

**Question**: Why won't fact-checking alone change this belief?

### Your answer should include:

1. **What functions** the belief serves
2. **Why facts don't address** those functions
3. **What intervention might** address the underlying needs

---

**Penalty for**: "They're just crazy," dismissal as analysis
**Reward for**: Function identification, need-aware intervention design
