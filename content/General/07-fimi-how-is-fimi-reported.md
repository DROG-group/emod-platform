## Purpose

You'll learn how different organisations report FIMI—and why standardisation matters for comparability and response.

---

## Scenario

You're reviewing FIMI reports from multiple sources. They use different frameworks, metrics, and definitions. You need to synthesise them. You have:

- **Time available**: Report analysis
- **Stakes**: Non-comparable reports limit collective response
- **Data access**: Reports from multiple organisations
- **Constraint**: No universal standard exists yet

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What frameworks are being used and how do they differ?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High in synthesising across reports
3. **Next action**: How to use non-standardised reports usefully?

---

## Method: Report Framework Analysis

### Start here: Identify the framework

Different organisations use different approaches:
- **EEAS/EU**: Standardised FIMI framework
- **Academic researchers**: Various methodologies
- **Platforms**: Coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) framing
- **Think tanks**: Varied approaches

### Step 2: Map key dimensions

Most reports address:
- **Actors**: Who (attributed or unattributed)
- **Behaviors**: What tactics were used
- **Content**: What narratives were pushed
- **Impact**: What effects were achieved

But they measure these differently.

### Step 3: Assess comparability

- Are definitions aligned?
- Are metrics comparable?
- Are confidence levels reported?
- Can you aggregate across reports?

---

## Major Reporting Frameworks

**EEAS (EU) Framework**:
- Standardised dimensions: severity, duration, impact
- Threat actors: technical/political attribution
- Motivation assessment
- Behaviors mapped to DISARM framework
- Database: OpenCTI

**Platform Reports** (Meta, Google, etc.):
- Focus on CIB (Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior)
- Account-level metrics
- Attribution when confident
- Removal statistics

**Academic/Think Tank**:
- Varied methodologies
- Often deeper analysis, narrower scope
- May have different evidence thresholds

**Government Intelligence**:
- Classified and unclassified versions
- Attribution confidence levels
- Often limited methodological transparency

---

## Standardisation Challenges

**Why standardisation matters**:
- Enables comparison across incidents
- Supports trend analysis
- Facilitates information sharing
- Makes collective response possible

**Why it's hard**:
- Different mandates (security vs research vs platform)
- Different access (classified vs open source)
- Different evidence thresholds
- Different definitions of key terms

**Current state**: Standardisation efforts ongoing (DISARM, EEAS) but not universal.

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong report synthesis**:
- Frameworks identified
- Comparability assessed
- Limitations acknowledged
- Appropriate caveats on aggregation

**Medium synthesis**:
- Some framework comparison
- Partial comparability
- Limited caveats

**Weak synthesis**:
- Mixing frameworks without awareness
- Assuming comparability
- No methodology assessment

---

## Stop Rule

Stop synthesising when:
- **Frameworks incompatible**: Can't meaningfully compare
- **Definitions conflict**: Same term means different things
- **Better to present separately**: Synthesis would mislead

---

## DIM Mapping

Reporting frameworks inform all DIM generations:

**Standardised reporting enables**:
- Trend detection (what's increasing?)
- Pattern recognition (what tactics repeat?)
- Response coordination (shared understanding)
- Effectiveness measurement (did interventions work?)

---

## Key Takeaway

FIMI reporting frameworks vary across organisations. Standardisation efforts are ongoing but incomplete. When using multiple reports, assess framework comparability before synthesising. Acknowledge limitations when aggregating across different methodologies.
