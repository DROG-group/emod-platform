## Purpose

You'll learn to shift the frame from "wrong beliefs" to "transactions that clear." Disinformation spreads not because people are gullible, but because the trade works for someone.

**Output format**: Assessment → Confidence (low/med/high) → Next action

---

## Scenario

A viral health claim is spreading across platforms. Your team wants to "debunk it." Before you do:

- **Time available**: 30 minutes to reframe the problem
- **Stakes**: If you misdiagnose, your intervention may feed the spread
- **Data access**: Content samples, engagement metrics, monetisation signals
- **Constraint**: You don't yet know who benefits

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What trade does this content enable, and for whom?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: Investigate the transaction, not just the claim

## Reality Check

Disinformation spreads because it clears trades: attention for status, outrage for belonging, clicks for money. Truth is optional.

This isn't a moral observation. It's a market observation. Understanding the market is the first step to intervening in it.

The content isn't the product. The transaction is.

---

## The Bedtime Story

"If people knew the facts, they'd believe differently."

This is comforting. It's also largely wrong.

Markets don't care. They price behaviour, not correctness. People don't consume information to be correct — they consume it to feel connected, informed, validated, or entertained.

The assumption that truth wins is itself a kind of misinformation about how information ecosystems work. If fact-based information competed on pure merit, cable news would look very different.

---

## Product vs. By-product

Here's the framing shift:

Often the "content" isn't the product. It's the by-product.

**The actual products:**
- Engagement (keeps you on platform)
- Recruitment (builds community or customer base)
- Donations (political or ideological fundraising)
- Authority (positions creator as expert)
- Distraction (pulls attention from something else)

The false claim is just the delivery vehicle. Ask: What's actually being sold here? If it's not truth, what is it?

---

## First Question

Stop asking: "Is it true?"

Start asking: "What trade does this enable, and for whom?"

This reframes the problem. You're not fact-checking beliefs — you're analysing transactions.

Who is giving what? Who is getting what? What market does this content serve?

This single reframe changes your entire analytical approach.

---

## Who Profits

Multiple actors extract value from the disinformation ecosystem:

**Creators**: Attention, followers, influence, money, authority
**Platforms**: Engagement, time-on-site, ad revenue, data
**Campaigners**: Political or ideological gains, recruitment, donations
**Opportunists**: Financial exploitation of attention, affiliate revenue
**The "counter" ecosystem**: Sometimes even fact-checkers and researchers extract value from the problem's persistence

This isn't cynicism. It's incentive mapping. Everyone in the system is responding to incentives.

---

## Who Pays

Costs are distributed, often to those who didn't choose to participate:

**Social trust**: Degraded by competing "truths"
**Institutional legitimacy**: Eroded when authorities are routinely questioned
**Public attention**: Consumed by noise instead of signal
**Security budgets**: Organisations pay for defensive measures
**Mental health**: Anxiety, confusion, polarisation
**Democratic processes**: Degraded decision-making capacity

The bill goes downstream to people who never clicked. The people making money rarely pay the costs they create.

---

## Mapping the Transaction

A useful exercise: For any piece of viral content, write the transaction:

**Who gives**:
- Audience: attention, engagement, shares, time
- Platform: reach, algorithmic amplification
- Advertisers: money (often unknowingly adjacent)

**Who gets**:
- Creator: followers, revenue, authority
- Platform: engagement metrics, ad impressions
- Campaign: recruitment, mobilisation

**How**:
- Algorithmic recommendation
- Social sharing
- Outrage-driven engagement
- Identity reinforcement

If you can write this ledger, you understand the market.

---

## Confidence Discipline

A critical distinction:

You can be **high-confidence** on the market framing while **low-confidence** on attribution.

"This content serves an engagement market and generates ad revenue" — assessable with public data.

"This content was created by Russian intelligence" — requires entirely different evidence.

Don't mix them. Market analysis doesn't require knowing who's behind it. You can understand why something spreads without proving who started it.

---

## The Incentive Map

For any disinformation ecosystem, map:

**Revenue streams**: Where does money flow? Ads, donations, subscriptions, affiliate links?
**Attention flows**: What captures engagement? Outrage, fear, identity, entertainment?
**Social capital**: Who gains status? Who loses it?
**Political capital**: Who gains power? Whose agenda advances?

The incentive map tells you where intervention might work. Change the incentives, change the behaviour.

---

## When Markets Fail

Markets in information "work" in the sense that transactions clear. They fail in the sense that:

**Externalities aren't priced**: The costs of disinformation fall on people who don't participate in the transaction

**Information asymmetry**: Buyers (audiences) often don't know what they're buying

**Monopoly dynamics**: A few platforms control distribution

**Attention is zero-sum**: Time spent on disinformation is time not spent on better information

These market failures are why intervention is sometimes justified. Pure market solutions won't work because the market itself is broken.

---

## DIM Application

The market framing informs response selection:

**If amplification is the harm channel:**
→ Gen 4 (moderation, reach reduction)
→ Target the distribution, not the belief

**If persuasion is the channel:**
→ Gen 3 (prebunking) or Gen 5 (community resilience)
→ Work on the demand side

**If it's noise without significant impact:**
→ Sometimes "do nothing" wins
→ Response can create the market that wasn't there

Detection is not the intervention. It's input into choosing one.

---

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: "People are gullible"**
This isn't an explanation. It's giving up on explanation. People respond to incentives and social contexts.

**Mistake 2: "Educate harder"**
If the market rewards disinformation, more education won't fix it. You need to change the market.

**Mistake 3: "Just debunk it"**
Debunking can add distribution. If you don't understand the transaction, you may be feeding it.

**Mistake 4: Attribution as goal**
Knowing who started something is often less useful than knowing why it spreads.

## Practical Exercise

**Task (20 minutes):**

Pick any viral claim currently circulating. Write analysis covering:

1. **Transaction framing** (2-3 sentences): What trade is this? Who gives what to whom?

2. **Who profits / who pays** (4-6 bullets): Map the value extraction and cost distribution

3. **Two hypotheses**: 
   - Hypothesis A: Organic spread driven by genuine belief
   - Hypothesis B: Incentivised spread driven by commercial/political interest
   
4. **Distinguishing evidence**: What would you look for to distinguish A from B?

5. **One incentive lever**: What change to the market would reduce spread?

6. **Assessment + Confidence + Next action**

---

## Sample Analysis

"Anti-vaccine content spreading on platform X"

**Transaction**: Audience gives attention and engagement; creator gets followers and authority; platform gets time-on-site; health product advertisers sometimes get sales.

**Who profits**: Creator (influence, possibly affiliate revenue), platform (engagement), alternative health industry (product sales)

**Who pays**: Public health (vaccine hesitancy), healthcare system (treating preventable illness), individuals (health outcomes)

**Hypotheses**: 
- A: Genuine health anxiety finding community
- B: Commercial operation monetising fear

**Distinguishing evidence**: Check for affiliate links, product sales, monetisation patterns

**Incentive lever**: Demonetisation of health misinformation; platform recommendation changes

**Assessment**: Market-driven health misinformation with mixed organic/commercial spread. Confidence: Medium. Next action: Map monetisation before planning intervention.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Disinformation spreads because it clears trades, not because people are stupid
- "Is it true?" matters less than "What trade does this enable?"
- Identify who profits and who pays — follow the incentives
- Market analysis doesn't require attribution — you can be confident about economics while uncertain about actors
- Response should target the market structure, not just the beliefs
- Sometimes the best intervention is changing what pays
- Detection is input for intervention selection, not the goal itself

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: A network of accounts is spreading climate denial content. Some accounts have large followings; others are smaller amplifiers. The content mixes genuine scepticism with industry talking points.

**Task (15 minutes):**

1. Write the transaction: Who gives what to get what?
2. Identify 3 potential profit streams (who might be paying for this?)
3. Identify 3 downstream costs (who pays without participating?)
4. What evidence would distinguish grassroots scepticism from funded campaign?
5. Propose one market-level intervention
6. Assessment + Confidence + Next action

**Scoring guide:**
- Penalise "people are gullible" explanations
- Reward transaction clarity
- Credit distinguishing market confidence from attribution confidence
