## Purpose

You'll learn to debunk effectively when debunking is appropriate—focusing on what research shows works, not intuitions that backfire.

## Scenario

A false claim has spread widely. You've determined debunking is the right approach (not amplification risk, audience is reachable). Now you need to do it effectively. You have:

- **Time available**: 2 hours to prepare correction
- **Stakes**: Poor debunking can reinforce the myth
- **Data access**: The false claim, evidence for correction
- **Constraint**: Correction competes with more engaging misinformation

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What makes this claim debunkable?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High that debunking will work
3. **Next action**: Specific debunking design following best practices

## Method: Effective Debunking Design

### Start here: Lead with truth, not myth

**Old approach (avoid)**: "Myth: X. Fact: Actually Y."
**Better approach**: Lead with the accurate information. Don't repeat the myth more than necessary.

Why: Repetition strengthens familiarity. Familiar feels true. Don't inadvertently reinforce what you're correcting.

### Step 2: Fill the gap

Corrections work better when they:
- Explain why the false claim exists
- Provide an alternative explanation
- Don't just say "wrong"—say what's actually true

**The gap**: When you remove a false belief, something needs to fill the space. Provide it.

### Step 3: Consider the audience

- **Who are you trying to reach?** (Persuadable middle, not committed believers)
- **What values do they hold?** (Frame correction to fit)
- **Who is a credible messenger?** (Source matters as much as content)

---

## Debunking Research Findings

**What works**:
- Leading with truth over myth
- Providing alternative explanation
- Using credible messengers (ideally in-group)
- Repetition of correction (not myth)
- Visual aids and clear formatting

**What backfires**:
- Repeating myth prominently
- Overwhelming with information
- Attacking identity instead of claim
- Using out-group messengers for in-group audiences

**Backfire effect reality check**:
- Less common than once claimed
- Happens mainly when identity is threatened
- Most corrections don't backfire—they just don't spread as far

---

## The "Truth Sandwich"

**Structure**:
1. **Lead with truth**: State the accurate information first
2. **Warn about the myth**: Brief mention that false claim exists
3. **Explain the fallacy**: Why the myth is wrong
4. **Return to truth**: Reinforce accurate information

**Why it works**: Truth gets the repetition. Myth gets minimal exposure with explicit flagging.

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (debunking will help):
- Claim is factual and clearly falsifiable
- Audience is not identity-committed to the claim
- Correction can reach same audience as original
- Credible messenger available

**Medium signals** (may help, limited):
- Claim involves values, not just facts
- Audience partially committed
- Correction will reach smaller audience

**Weak signals** (debunking may not work):
- Claim is identity-defining for believers
- Audience hostile to any correction
- No credible messenger available

---

## Stop Rule

Stop debunking when:
- **Identity threat too high**: Correction threatens core identity
- **Audience unreachable**: Better to focus on persuadable observers
- **Amplification risk**: Correction draws more attention to myth
- **Resources exceeded**: Can't compete with disinformation volume

---

## DIM Mapping

Debunking is Gen 2 intervention:

**Gen 2 strengths**: Direct correction of specific claims
**Gen 2 limits**: Reactive, slow, doesn't scale, correction spreads less

**When to use Gen 2**:
- Claim is already established
- High-stakes factual claim
- Persuadable audience exists

**Combine with**:
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): Prevent future similar claims
- **Gen 4** (Moderation): Reduce myth visibility
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Make correction more discoverable

---

## Key Takeaway

Debunking works when done right. Lead with truth, fill the gap, use credible messengers, don't repeat the myth unnecessarily. Know the limits: some audiences won't be persuaded, some claims aren't worth amplifying, some resources are better spent on prevention.
