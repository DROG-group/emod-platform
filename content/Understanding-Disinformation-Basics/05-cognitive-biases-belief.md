## Method: Bias Assessment Workflow

### Start here: Assume rationality, then identify constraints

Before diagnosing irrationality:
- People usually have reasons for their beliefs
- "Irrational" beliefs often serve identity or social functions
- Cognitive shortcuts usually work—they're bugs in specific contexts

**Key frame**: People aren't stupid. They're using heuristics that fail in information-rich, adversarial environments.

### Step 2: Identify relevant biases

Which cognitive patterns explain the belief persistence?

**Information processing**:
- Confirmation bias: Seeking/accepting belief-consistent information
- Availability heuristic: Overweighting vivid or recent examples
- Anchoring: First information disproportionately influences judgement

**Social and identity**:
- Social proof: Believing what peers believe
- Identity-protective cognition: Rejecting information threatening to identity
- Tribal epistemics: Evaluating claims by who says them, not evidence

**Correction resistance**:
- Backfire effect: Corrections sometimes strengthen beliefs (though rarer than claimed)
- Continued influence: Retracted information continues affecting reasoning
- Fluency: Familiar claims feel more true

### Step 3: Check your own biases

You're not immune:
- Are you assuming the audience is uniquely biased?
- Could your assessment reflect your own confirmation bias?
- Are you in your own information environment?

## Key Biases Reference

**Confirmation bias**:
- We seek information confirming existing beliefs
- We interpret ambiguous information as supportive
- Not: stupidity. This is efficient most of the time.

**Motivated reasoning**:
- We reason toward conclusions we want
- Stronger for identity-relevant topics
- Not: intentional deception. It's usually unconscious.

**Availability heuristic**:
- Easily recalled examples seem more common
- Vivid stories outweigh statistics
- Explains why rare dramatic events feel common

**Social proof**:
- We believe what our community believes
- Stronger in uncertainty
- Legitimate heuristic that can be exploited

**Identity-protective cognition**:
- Information threatening identity gets rejected
- Scientific literacy can increase polarisation on identity topics
- Explains why "more facts" doesn't always help

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (bias-driven belief):
- Belief correlates strongly with identity group
- Evidence quality doesn't predict belief (only source does)
- Corrections produce defensive responses
- Belief serves clear psychological or social function

**Medium signals** (bias may be a factor):
- Selective engagement with evidence
- In-group sources trusted more than out-group
- Emotional investment in the belief

**Weak signals** (insufficient for bias diagnosis):
- Person believes something you think is wrong
- Person doesn't change mind when presented evidence
- Person is in different information environment

## Case Study 1: Vaccine Hesitancy Psychology

### Understanding vaccine concerns through cognitive lens

Vaccine hesitancy provides a useful case for understanding how cognitive biases interact with real-world belief formation. This analysis does not aim to pathologise those with concerns—many hesitant individuals have coherent reasons rooted in personal experience, community norms, or reasonable uncertainty about novel interventions.

**What the research shows** (medium-high confidence):

Vaccine hesitancy correlates with several identifiable cognitive patterns:

**Availability heuristic in action**: Stories of adverse events—whether verified or anecdotal—carry disproportionate weight compared to statistical safety data. A single vivid account from a trusted community member can outweigh aggregate data from millions of doses. This isn't irrational; our brains evolved to learn from specific experiences, not population-level statistics.

**Omission bias**: Research suggests people evaluate harms from action (getting vaccinated) more severely than equivalent harms from inaction (remaining unvaccinated). The psychological burden of "causing" an adverse event through choice feels different from "allowing" harm through inaction, even when outcomes are statistically equivalent.

**Trust heuristics**: In environments where institutional trust is low—whether from historical experience or current polarisation—people rely more heavily on in-group sources. This is adaptive behaviour in contexts where institutions have previously failed communities.

### Why facts alone don't change minds

Multiple studies have examined why factual information about vaccine safety has limited persuasive effect among hesitant populations:

**Identity threat responses**: When vaccination becomes associated with political or cultural identity, safety information can be processed as an attack on identity rather than neutral data. The messenger matters as much as the message.

**Source credibility gaps**: Information from institutions perceived as untrustworthy—regardless of factual accuracy—faces immediate discounting. This is compounded when those institutions have histories of genuine failures in specific communities.

**Reactance effects**: Heavy-handed messaging emphasising that hesitancy is "wrong" or "dangerous" can trigger defensive responses. Autonomy threats tend to produce resistance, not compliance.

### What approaches showed better results

Research on effective vaccine communication (confidence level: medium) suggests several approaches:

**Motivational interviewing**: Approaches that explore concerns without dismissing them, acknowledge uncertainty where it exists, and support autonomous decision-making showed more consistent effects than direct persuasion attempts.

**Trusted messenger effects**: Information delivered by sources already trusted by hesitant communities—whether healthcare providers with established relationships, community leaders, or peers—showed stronger effects than institutional messaging.

**Addressing specific concerns**: Generic "vaccines are safe" messaging performed worse than targeted responses to specific concerns. Different populations had different primary concerns requiring different informational approaches.

**What didn't work well**: Shaming, implying irrationality, or framing hesitancy as a moral failing generally produced defensive responses rather than attitude change.

**Key lesson**: The cognitive patterns underlying hesitancy are normal human psychology, not defects. Interventions that work with these patterns—respecting autonomy, using trusted messengers, addressing specific concerns—outperform approaches that treat hesitancy as a knowledge deficit to be corrected.

## Case Study 2: Election Fraud Belief Persistence

### How beliefs persisted despite debunking

Following contested elections in multiple democracies, significant portions of populations maintained beliefs in widespread fraud despite extensive debunking efforts, court rulings, and official certifications. This pattern offers insights into belief persistence mechanisms.

**Documented patterns** (high confidence these occurred; lower confidence on precise causal mechanisms):

**Pre-existing narrative frameworks**: In cases where fraud allegations found fertile ground, audiences had often been primed with "elections can be stolen" narratives for months or years before the specific election. The fraud claims fit into existing mental models rather than introducing new concepts.

**Social proof at scale**: When significant portions of one's social network express belief in fraud, individual belief becomes socially reinforced. Changing one's mind requires not only processing new information but potentially disrupting important social relationships.

**Information environment effects**: Populations with primary exposure to sources affirming fraud claims received limited exposure to debunking information. When debunks did penetrate, they often came from sources already distrusted by the audience.

### Identity-protective cognition in action

Election fraud beliefs demonstrate identity-protective cognition at scale:

**What identity protection looks like**: For many believers, acknowledging fraud claims were unfounded required accepting that their preferred candidate legitimately lost, that sources they trusted were unreliable, and potentially that they had been deceived. This represents significant identity threat across multiple dimensions.

**The asymmetric scrutiny pattern**: Research on motivated reasoning shows people apply more scrutiny to information challenging their beliefs than to information supporting them. Fraud believers subjected debunking to extensive critique while accepting supporting claims with less examination.

**Community reinforcement**: In communities where fraud belief became normative, expressing doubt about the claims carried social costs. Belief maintenance became partially independent of evidence evaluation.

### Lessons for intervention design

This case suggests several principles for addressing identity-linked beliefs:

**Timing matters**: Interventions before beliefs become identity-attached have different dynamics than interventions after. Pre-bunking approaches that prepare audiences for manipulation attempts may be more effective than post-hoc debunking.

**Messenger selection is critical**: Corrections from out-group sources may strengthen rather than weaken beliefs. Effective correction may require messengers who share important identity dimensions with the audience.

**Address the identity threat**: Approaches that allow people to change beliefs without feeling they were "fooled" or that their identity group was wrong may reduce resistance. This might include framing that acknowledges how the misinformation was designed to be convincing.

**Acknowledge limits**: For deeply identity-attached beliefs in highly polarised environments, available interventions may have modest effects. Recognising these limits helps set realistic expectations and avoid counterproductive escalation.

## Tool Tutorial: Cognitive Assessment Framework

### Identifying which biases are at play

When analysing belief persistence, systematic assessment helps distinguish which cognitive factors are most relevant. This matters because different biases suggest different intervention approaches.

**Assessment questions to work through**:

**1. Information processing patterns**
- How did the person encounter this belief? (Availability)
- What was their first exposure? (Anchoring)
- What sources do they cite as authoritative? (Source heuristics)
- Do they actively seek confirming information? (Confirmation bias)

**2. Social and identity factors**
- Does belief correlate with group membership?
- Would changing the belief create social costs?
- Is the belief tied to broader identity commitments?
- Who in their network shares or challenges the belief?

**3. Emotional and motivational factors**
- Does the belief serve psychological needs (control, meaning, belonging)?
- Is there emotional investment beyond mere opinion?
- Does challenging the belief trigger defensive responses?

**4. Information environment factors**
- What sources dominate their information diet?
- Do they encounter diverse perspectives?
- How much exposure have they had to counter-information?

### Avoiding the "they're biased, I'm not" trap

The most common error in cognitive assessment is asymmetric application—seeing biases clearly in others while remaining blind to one's own.

**Self-check protocol**:

**Before diagnosing others' biases, ask**:
- Would I apply the same analysis to beliefs I share?
- Am I using "bias" to dismiss views I find inconvenient?
- Have I examined my own information sources with equal scrutiny?
- Could someone reasonably apply my diagnosis to me?

**Signs you may be falling into the trap**:
- You find biases mainly in out-group members
- Your analysis conveniently supports conclusions you already held
- You feel certain about your objectivity
- You haven't identified any biases in your own thinking recently

**Corrective practice**: Before completing any bias assessment of others, write down three ways the same cognitive patterns might affect your own analysis. If you cannot identify any, your assessment is likely compromised.

### Practical application

**Worked example**: A colleague believes that a particular policy will have catastrophic consequences, despite expert consensus suggesting more modest effects.

**Step 1 - Information processing assessment**:
- They encountered the claim through a vivid documentary (availability)
- Their first exposure was to extreme predictions (anchoring)
- They cite activist sources as more trustworthy than mainstream experts (source heuristics)

**Step 2 - Social/identity assessment**:
- Belief is common in their social network
- Challenging it might strain friendships
- The belief connects to broader values they hold about the policy area

**Step 3 - Self-check**:
- Am I dismissing their concern because I don't share their values?
- Have I actually examined the evidence they find compelling?
- Could they reasonably apply similar analysis to my own views?

**Step 4 - Intervention implications**:
- Direct factual confrontation likely to trigger defensiveness
- Might explore their underlying values and find common ground
- Could identify sources they trust who hold more moderate views
- Should acknowledge legitimate aspects of their concerns

## Practice Exercise 1: Intervention Design

### Scenario: Correcting a belief tied to identity

A community group believes that a local development project will destroy their neighborhood character. They cite a widely-shared social media post claiming the project will displace 80% of current residents. The actual planning documents show the figure is closer to 8%, and includes relocation assistance.

The community has historical reasons to distrust developers and local government. Previous projects in similar neighborhoods did result in significant displacement, though circumstances were different.

### Your task: Design a bias-aware intervention

**Consider**:

1. **Bias identification**: What cognitive patterns are operating?
   - Historical experience making current claims feel plausible (availability)
   - Community consensus reinforcing the belief (social proof)
   - Identity as "defenders of the neighborhood" (identity-protective cognition)
   - Distrust of official sources (source credibility)

2. **Intervention constraints**:
   - Direct contradiction may trigger defensive responses
   - The source of accurate information (developer, government) is distrusted
   - The community has legitimate historical grievances
   - Some uncertainty about actual outcomes exists

3. **Design your approach**:
   - Who should deliver the correction?
   - How should accurate information be framed?
   - What legitimate concerns should be acknowledged?
   - What would success look like?

### Common mistakes to avoid

**Mistake 1: Leading with the correction**
"The 80% figure is wrong; it's actually 8%." This frames the community as having believed something false, triggering identity defense.

**Better approach**: "Your concerns about displacement are understandable given what happened in [previous project]. Let's look at what's actually in this plan together."

**Mistake 2: Ignoring the trust problem**
Presenting official documents as authoritative when official sources are distrusted merely reinforces that you don't understand the community's perspective.

**Better approach**: Identify community members or independent parties who have reviewed the documents and can serve as trusted intermediaries.

**Mistake 3: Dismissing the underlying concern**
Focusing solely on the incorrect statistic while ignoring legitimate worries about neighborhood change misses why the false figure was compelling.

**Better approach**: Address both the specific inaccuracy AND the underlying concerns about community impact.

**Mistake 4: Assuming the correction ends the issue**
Even if the 80% figure is successfully corrected, underlying distrust and concern remain. A single correction doesn't rebuild trust.

**Better approach**: View the correction as one element of longer-term engagement, not a one-time fix.

## Practice Exercise 2: Self-Assessment

### Examining your own potential biases

This exercise asks you to apply the cognitive assessment framework to your own beliefs. Select a topic where you hold views that others in your professional or social environment might challenge.

**Step 1: Identify a belief to examine**

Choose something you feel confident about but that reasonable people disagree on. Avoid trivially verifiable facts; focus on interpretations, predictions, or value-laden assessments.

Examples:
- A policy position you support
- An assessment of a public figure
- A prediction about future events
- An interpretation of contested evidence

**Step 2: Apply the assessment questions**

**Information sources**:
- Where did you first encounter this view?
- What sources do you rely on for information about this topic?
- When did you last seriously engage with opposing perspectives?
- How would you characterise the information environment you inhabit on this topic?

**Social factors**:
- How common is this view in your social and professional networks?
- What social costs would you face for changing your mind?
- Is this view associated with groups you identify with?

**Emotional factors**:
- How do you feel when this view is challenged?
- Does holding this view serve psychological needs (feeling informed, being on the right side, belonging)?
- How certain do you feel, and is that certainty proportionate to the evidence?

**Step 3: Honestly evaluate the results**

If your assessment reveals:
- You learned this view from in-group sources and rarely engage opposing views
- Holding it carries social rewards and changing it would have social costs
- You feel defensive when it's challenged
- Your certainty exceeds what the evidence strictly supports

...then you have identified conditions where bias may be influencing your belief. This doesn't mean the belief is wrong—biased processes can reach correct conclusions. But it does mean you should hold the belief with appropriate humility and remain open to revision.

### Applying symmetric standards

**The symmetry test**: Would you accept the reasoning that supports your belief if it were offered by someone on the "other side" of the issue?

**Example**: If you believe a study supporting your view is methodologically sound, would you accept the same methodology if the results contradicted your view?

**Example**: If you dismiss opposing sources as biased, do you apply the same scrutiny to sources you agree with?

**Example**: If you find anecdotes supporting your view compelling, do you find opposing anecdotes equally compelling?

Failure of the symmetry test doesn't prove your belief is wrong, but it does indicate that your reasoning process may be contaminated by motivated cognition. The appropriate response is increased humility, not necessarily belief change—but genuine openness to revision.

## Regional Focus: Cultural Variations in Trust

### How trust in institutions varies across EU

Trust patterns vary significantly across European countries, with implications for how cognitive biases manifest and how interventions should be designed.

**Documented trust patterns** (based on Eurobarometer and similar surveys; medium-high confidence in the pattern existence, lower confidence in precise causal explanations):

**Northern/Western Europe**: Generally higher trust in national institutions, media, and scientific bodies. Populations more receptive to institutional messaging but potentially vulnerable to overconfidence in official sources.

**Southern Europe**: More variable trust patterns, often with distinctions between specific institutions. May show higher trust in EU institutions than national ones in some contexts.

**Central/Eastern Europe**: Often lower trust in national institutions, sometimes reflecting historical experience with state propaganda. May show higher skepticism toward official narratives—which can be both protective (against state manipulation) and vulnerability (to anti-institutional disinformation).

**Cross-cutting factors**: Urban/rural divides, age demographics, and education levels create variation within countries that can exceed between-country differences.

### Cultural factors affecting belief formation

**Individualism vs. collectivism dimensions**: Research suggests cultures emphasising individual judgment may show different bias patterns than those emphasising collective wisdom. Interventions leveraging social proof may work differently across these dimensions.

**Historical experience with propaganda**: Populations with living memory of state propaganda may show different responses to official corrections—both more skeptical of official narratives and more sophisticated about manipulation techniques.

**Media landscape effects**: Countries with strong public broadcasting traditions show different trust patterns than those with more fragmented media environments. This affects which sources can serve as credible messengers for corrections.

**Religious and cultural authority patterns**: The role of religious institutions, community elders, and other traditional authority sources varies significantly and affects which messengers carry credibility.

### Implications for interventions

**Adapt messenger selection**: An intervention using government health authorities as messengers may work well in high-trust environments but poorly in low-trust ones. The same intervention might need community leaders, local doctors, or other trusted figures in different contexts.

**Adjust institutional framing**: In low-trust environments, framing corrections as coming from "official sources" may reduce rather than enhance credibility. Consider framing that emphasises transparency, acknowledges past failures, or uses non-institutional messengers.

**Account for historical context**: Interventions that seem patronising ("trust us, we know better") may trigger particularly negative responses in populations with historical reasons to distrust authorities.

**Test before scaling**: What works in one cultural context may fail or backfire in another. Pilot interventions with target populations before broader deployment.

## Expert Perspective: Cognitive Science Research

### Key findings on bias and belief

Cognitive science research on bias and belief change has evolved considerably. Here are findings with relevance for practitioners, with confidence levels noted:

**High confidence findings**:

**Biases are universal**: Cognitive biases appear across all studied populations, education levels, and intelligence levels. Expertise in a domain doesn't eliminate bias; it may even increase overconfidence in that domain.

**Biases often serve useful functions**: Most cognitive shortcuts work well in ordinary circumstances. They become problematic in specific contexts: information-rich environments, adversarial settings, topics far from direct experience.

**Correction difficulty increases with identity attachment**: The more a belief becomes tied to identity, the more resistant it becomes to evidence-based correction. Early intervention (before identity attachment) differs fundamentally from late intervention.

**Medium confidence findings**:

**Motivated reasoning is largely unconscious**: People engaging in motivated reasoning typically don't experience themselves as biased; they feel they're following the evidence. This has implications for interventions—appeals to "be unbiased" may be ineffective because people don't perceive themselves as biased.

**Source credibility moderates correction effectiveness**: The same correction is more effective when delivered by sources the audience trusts. This effect is stronger than message content effects in many studies.

**Repetition increases perceived truth**: The "illusory truth effect" shows that repeated exposure to claims increases their perceived truthfulness, independent of actual accuracy. This has implications for both spreading and correcting false claims.

### The backfire effect debate

The "backfire effect"—corrections strengthening rather than weakening false beliefs—received significant attention after initial studies suggested it was common. Subsequent research has complicated this picture:

**Current understanding** (medium confidence):

**Backfire is rarer than initially claimed**: Large-scale replication attempts found backfire effects to be uncommon. Most corrections produce either belief change in the correct direction or no change—not strengthening of the false belief.

**Context matters significantly**: Backfire may be more likely under specific conditions: highly identity-relevant topics, when corrections are delivered by distrusted sources, when framing triggers defensive responses.

**Attitude vs. belief distinction**: Some studies suggest corrections may update factual beliefs while leaving attitudes unchanged or even entrenched. This matters for practitioner expectations about what "success" looks like.

**Practical implication**: Fear of backfire should not prevent corrections, but corrections should be designed to minimise identity threat and defensive responses. The risk of backfire is real but limited; the risk of uncorrected misinformation is more common.

### What practitioners should know

**Design implications from research**:

**Work with biases, not against them**: Instead of trying to eliminate biases (which is unrealistic), design interventions that leverage the same cognitive mechanisms toward accurate beliefs.

**Messengers matter as much as messages**: Invest in identifying and engaging credible messengers for your target audience, not only in crafting accurate messages.

**Timing matters**: Pre-bunking (before false beliefs form) and early intervention (before identity attachment) are generally more effective than correction of established beliefs.

**Set realistic expectations**: Even well-designed interventions may have modest effects on strongly-held, identity-linked beliefs. This doesn't mean interventions are worthless; it means expectations should be calibrated to evidence.

**Maintain humility**: The same biases you're trying to correct in others affect your own analysis. Build in checks against your own motivated reasoning.

## Historical Context: Understanding Belief Through History

### Historical examples of mass belief

History provides cases of widespread belief persistence that illuminate cognitive patterns, though historical cases require caution—we know outcomes but may misattribute causes.

**Medical history examples**:

**Bloodletting persistence**: The practice of bloodletting persisted for centuries despite limited evidence of efficacy and substantial evidence of harm. Multiple cognitive factors sustained it: authority deference (endorsed by respected physicians), confirmation bias (patients who recovered confirmed efficacy; those who died were "too far gone"), and social proof (widespread practice normalised it).

**Semmelweis and handwashing**: When Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated that handwashing dramatically reduced childbed fever deaths, the medical establishment rejected his findings for decades. His evidence challenged physicians' identity (the idea that "gentlemen's hands could not carry disease") and contradicted established theory.

**What these cases suggest**: Expertise and education don't immunise against bias. Institutional resistance to evidence can persist even when lives are at stake. Social and identity factors can override empirical observation.

**Political and social examples**:

**Propaganda effectiveness in authoritarian regimes**: Studies of belief under authoritarian propaganda suggest sophisticated populations can maintain private skepticism while publicly conforming, but prolonged exposure combined with social enforcement can shift genuine private beliefs over time.

**Financial bubbles**: Repeated patterns of widespread belief in unsustainable valuations (tulip mania, South Sea Bubble, 2008 housing crisis) demonstrate how social proof and motivated reasoning can produce mass belief that contradicts fundamental analysis.

### What we've learned about cognition

**Historical cases support several contemporary findings**:

**Intelligent people are not immune**: Many historical examples involve well-educated individuals and established experts. Intelligence provides more sophisticated rationalisation, not immunity from bias.

**Social factors are powerful**: Beliefs reinforced by social networks, professional identity, and authority structures are particularly persistent. Individual cognitive correction faces social-level resistance.

**New information alone is insufficient**: Semmelweis had the data. So did early proponents of germ theory. Data availability doesn't guarantee belief change when other factors resist it.

**Paradigm shifts take time**: Major belief changes often require generational turnover ("science advances one funeral at a time") rather than real-time persuasion. This suggests patience and persistence in long-term interventions.

### Application to modern disinformation

**Historical perspective on current challenges**:

**Not unprecedented**: While scale and speed are new, resistance to evidence and persistence of false beliefs are not. This should temper both alarm ("we've never faced anything like this") and resignation ("nothing works").

**Structural factors matter**: Historical cases where beliefs eventually changed often involved structural changes (new institutions, professional norms, educational reforms) not only individual persuasion. This suggests attention to information environment design, not only individual correction.

**Time horizons**: If historical paradigm shifts took decades to generations, modern expectations of rapid belief change may be unrealistic. Interventions should be evaluated on appropriate timescales.

**Caveat**: Historical analogies are useful for perspective but imperfect guides to action. The information environment, social structures, and available interventions differ significantly from historical cases.

## Quick Reference: Bias-Aware Intervention Checklist

### Before designing an intervention

**Assessment phase**:
- [ ] Have I identified specific cognitive factors at play (not generic "bias")?
- [ ] Have I assessed whether the belief is identity-attached?
- [ ] Have I examined the target audience's information environment?
- [ ] Have I identified who they consider credible sources?
- [ ] Have I applied the same bias analysis to my own perspective?

### Designing the intervention

**Messenger selection**:
- [ ] Is the messenger credible to the target audience (not to me)?
- [ ] Have I considered identity match between messenger and audience?
- [ ] Have I avoided sources the audience actively distrusts?

**Message framing**:
- [ ] Does the framing avoid triggering identity threat?
- [ ] Have I acknowledged legitimate concerns or uncertainties?
- [ ] Does the correction fit with values the audience holds?
- [ ] Am I correcting the specific claim without dismissing underlying concerns?

**Avoiding common errors**:
- [ ] Am I working with cognitive patterns rather than against them?
- [ ] Have I avoided implying the audience is irrational or stupid?
- [ ] Am I respecting audience autonomy rather than demanding compliance?
- [ ] Have I set realistic expectations for the intervention's effects?

### After deploying the intervention

**Evaluation**:
- [ ] Did I measure effects on the actual target audience (not sympathetic observers)?
- [ ] Did I check for potential defensive responses or backfire?
- [ ] Did I assess whether belief changed even if behaviour didn't?
- [ ] Have I documented what worked and what didn't for future interventions?

**Self-reflection**:
- [ ] Am I updating my approach based on results or defending my initial design?
- [ ] Have I considered alternative explanations for observed effects?
- [ ] Am I maintaining appropriate humility about what the intervention achieved?

### Quick diagnostic questions

When planning any correction or intervention, ask:

1. **Who**: Who is the audience, and who do they trust?
2. **What**: What specific belief am I addressing, and what cognitive factors sustain it?
3. **Why**: Why might they resist correction (identity, social, informational factors)?
4. **How**: How can I frame this to work with rather than against their psychology?
5. **Me**: What are my own biases in this analysis, and have I checked them?

## Stop Rule

Stop bias analysis when:
- **You're pathologising disagreement**: Different conclusions from same evidence isn't always bias
- **Intervention doesn't change**: Understanding bias helps design, but sometimes you proceed regardless
- **You're avoiding the argument**: "They're biased" can be an excuse not to engage with their actual reasoning
- **Your bias is the issue**: Check whether you're diagnosing biases to avoid examining your own

## DIM Mapping

Bias understanding shapes intervention design:

**Confirmation bias**:
- **Gen 2** (Debunking): Frame corrections to fit existing values
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): Build habits of seeking disconfirming evidence

**Identity-protective cognition**:
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Use identity-congruent messengers
- Avoid framing that triggers identity threat
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): Build resilience before identity attachment forms

**Social proof**:
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Create visible communities holding accurate beliefs
- Counter fake social proof with authentic alternatives

**General principle**: Work with biases, not against them. Design interventions that make accurate beliefs identity-congruent, socially rewarded, and easy to process.

## Key Takeaway

Cognitive biases aren't character flaws—they're features that usually help but fail in adversarial information environments. Effective interventions don't assume people are stupid; they design for how cognition actually works. This includes acknowledging your own biases in the analysis.
