## Purpose

You'll learn to distinguish disinformation from misinformation and malinformation—not to win arguments, but to choose the right intervention.

---

## Scenario

Your team flags a viral post making false health claims. Before recommending action, you need to classify it. You have:

- **Time available**: 20 minutes for initial classification
- **Stakes**: Classification determines response type (correct someone vs counter a campaign)
- **Data access**: The post, account history, limited engagement data
- **Constraint**: Intent is hard to prove

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: Is this disinformation, misinformation, or malinformation?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: What intervention category does this suggest?

---

## Method: Classification Workflow

### Start here: Check factual accuracy

Before classifying intent:
- Is the core claim false or misleading?
- Can you verify against reliable sources?

**If content is accurate** → Stop. This isn't a dis/mis/mal-information problem.
**If content is false or misleading** → Move to intent assessment.

### Step 2: Assess intent signals

Look for indicators of deliberate deception:
- Account history (pattern of false claims?)
- Source sophistication (amateur mistake vs professional production?)
- Coordination signals (others posting identical content?)

**Strong signal of intent**: Professional production + coordination + account history of false claims
**Medium signal**: Some indicators but could be genuine error
**Weak signal**: "It feels intentional" without evidence

### Step 3: Check for harm framing

Even accurate information can be weaponised:
- Is true information presented to mislead?
- Is private information exposed to cause harm?
- Is context stripped to distort meaning?

This is malinformation—true but harmful.

---

## The Definitions (Reference)

**Misinformation**: False information shared without intent to deceive. The sharer believes it's true.

**Disinformation**: False information deliberately created or spread to deceive. Intent matters.

**Malinformation**: True information shared to cause harm. Often private information or out-of-context facts.

**Key insight**: The same content can be misinformation for one sharer and disinformation for another. Intent travels with the actor, not the content.

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (disinformation likely):
- Coordinated spread across multiple accounts
- Professional production quality inconsistent with claimed source
- Account has history of deliberate false claims
- Content designed to exploit known biases

**Medium signals** (investigate further):
- High engagement but unclear origin
- Mixes true and false elements
- Account is new or has limited history

**Weak signals** (insufficient alone):
- Content is false (doesn't prove intent)
- Poster seems suspicious (vibes aren't evidence)
- "Who benefits?" speculation

## Case Study 1: The 5G-COVID Conspiracy (2020)

### Background

In early 2020, as COVID-19 spread globally, a false narrative emerged linking 5G mobile network infrastructure to the pandemic. The claims varied: some suggested 5G radiation weakened immune systems, others claimed the virus spread through 5G signals, and more extreme versions asserted that COVID-19 was a cover story for 5G radiation sickness.

### Timeline: January-April 2020

**January 2020**: Early claims appear on conspiracy forums and fringe health sites, primarily in English-speaking countries. Low-quality videos and poorly sourced articles circulate in small communities.

**February 2020**: Claims begin crossing from fringe to mainstream social platforms. A Belgian doctor's interview mentioning 5G and health concerns (later retracted) gets amplified out of context.

**March 2020**: Significant acceleration. Celebrity endorsements bring claims to wider audiences. Hashtags trend in multiple countries. The narrative adapts to local contexts—different countries see localised versions.

**April 2020**: Physical consequences emerge. Dozens of mobile phone towers are vandalised or set on fire in the UK, Netherlands, and other countries. Telecom workers report harassment. Platforms begin more aggressive content removal.

### Analysis: Misinformation or Disinformation?

This case illustrates why binary classification is insufficient. Different actors, different intents:

**Misinformation spreaders** (high confidence):
- Concerned citizens sharing content they believed was true
- Parents worried about family health during a frightening pandemic
- People with pre-existing distrust of technology acting on genuine fears
- Signals: amateur sharing patterns, engagement in comments seeking information, diverse account histories

**Disinformation spreaders** (medium confidence):
- Accounts with history of health misinformation pivoting to new opportunity
- Some coordination patterns among anti-vaccine and alternative health networks
- Content creators monetising conspiracy content through ad revenue
- Signals: professional production, cross-platform coordination, monetisation patterns

**Uncertain classification** (requires more investigation):
- State-linked media amplification in some regions
- Whether coordination was ideological alignment or directed campaign
- Role of platform algorithms in spread versus intentional seeding

### Response: What Worked and What Did Not

**Platform action** (medium effectiveness):
- Content removal and labelling reduced some spread
- Limitations: whack-a-mole dynamic, content migrated to alternative platforms
- Lesson: Moderation can slow but not stop determined spreaders

**Debunking** (limited effectiveness):
- Fact-checks reached some audiences
- Limitations: those most committed to the narrative rejected fact-checks as part of the cover-up
- Lesson: Debunking works for undecided audiences, less effective for true believers

**Prebunking** (promising but late):
- Some media literacy content helped audiences recognise conspiracy patterns
- Limitation: arrived after narrative was established
- Lesson: Prebunking needs to be in place before crises

**What worked best**: Combination of platform action to slow spread, credible local voices (including telecom workers sharing personal experiences), and addressing underlying fears about pandemic uncertainty rather than attacking belief holders.

### Key Lessons for Classification

1. **The same content, different actors**: A single viral video might be disinformation at origin, misinformation for most sharers
2. **Classification affects intervention**: Correcting a worried parent differs from countering a monetised conspiracy channel
3. **Harm can occur regardless of intent**: Vandalised towers and harassed workers were harmed whether or not most sharers intended harm
4. **Attribution confidence matters**: Stating "this was a disinformation campaign" when evidence only supports "this contained misinformation that spread through organic and possibly coordinated means" is overclaiming

## Case Study 2: The Baltic Election Interference Attempt (2019)

### Background

In the lead-up to a Baltic state's parliamentary elections, documents appeared online purporting to show a prominent politician involved in financial misconduct. The documents were professionally formatted, included apparently official letterheads, and were released through an anonymous channel.

### Timeline: Leak, Amplification, Exposure

**Week 1 - The Leak**: Documents surface on an anonymous file-sharing platform. Initial sharing through accounts with limited history. Some social media accounts begin referencing the documents before mainstream discovery—a coordination signal.

**Week 2 - Amplification**: Fringe news sites pick up the story. Some accounts systematically tag journalists and political opponents. Cross-platform spread follows predictable patterns associated with influence operations.

**Week 3 - Exposure**: Local fact-checkers identify forensic inconsistencies in the documents. Metadata analysis suggests documents were created recently, not on dates shown. The letterhead contains subtle errors inconsistent with genuine government documents. Official denial accompanied by evidence of forgery.

**Week 4 - Attribution**: Security services assess with medium confidence that the operation had foreign state involvement, based on technical indicators and operational patterns consistent with previous attributed campaigns. The political impact is limited due to rapid exposure.

### Analysis: Clear Disinformation

Unlike Case Study 1, this case shows strong signals of deliberate deception:

**Strong signals present**:
- Professional document forgery requiring resources and expertise
- Coordinated release and amplification pattern
- Timing aligned with electoral calendar (harm-maximising)
- Accounts involved showed patterns consistent with previous attributed operations
- No plausible innocent explanation for fabricated documents

**Classification confidence**: High that this was disinformation at origin and in initial amplification. Some later spread may have been misinformation by users who believed documents were genuine.

**Attribution confidence**: Medium for foreign state involvement. Technical indicators are suggestive but not definitive. Pattern matching to previous campaigns is informative but not proof. Responsible communication acknowledges this uncertainty.

### Response: Rapid Prebunking

**What worked**:
- Local fact-checkers had established credibility and rapid-response capability
- Pre-existing relationships between journalists, fact-checkers, and election officials enabled fast coordination
- Forensic document analysis was communicated accessibly to general audiences
- The exposure narrative ("foreign interference attempt fails") may have reduced the political impact more than the documents' claims would have increased it

**Critical factors**:
- Speed: Exposure came before the narrative consolidated
- Credibility: Fact-checkers were trusted across political spectrum
- Preparation: Previous awareness campaigns about interference tactics created audience receptivity to the exposure framing

### Key Lessons for Classification

1. **Some cases are clearer than others**: Fabricated documents with coordination signals are easier to classify than ambiguous viral content
2. **Attribution confidence is separate from classification confidence**: You can be confident something is disinformation without being confident about who created it
3. **Response speed matters**: Rapid exposure before a narrative consolidates is more effective than debunking after widespread acceptance
4. **Preparation enables response**: Communities with prebunking exposure and trusted fact-checkers respond better to interference attempts

## Practice Exercise 1: Health Claim Classification

### Scenario

A viral social media post claims that a widely prescribed blood pressure medication causes significant heart attack risk. The post has been shared 50,000 times across platforms in 48 hours.

Your initial review reveals:

- **The claim**: Partially based on a real study, but significantly exaggerates the findings. The study showed a small statistical correlation requiring further research, not a causal relationship.
- **Original poster**: An account with 12,000 followers that primarily posts alternative health content. Account is 3 years old with consistent posting history.
- **Spread pattern**: You identify three distinct groups sharing the content:
  - Group A: Health-focused accounts with alternative medicine orientation, sharing with commentary like "I knew it!" and "They don't want you to know"
  - Group B: Personal accounts sharing with comments like "Has anyone else heard about this? Should I ask my doctor?"
  - Group C: A cluster of 15 accounts that shared nearly identical text within a 30-minute window, most created in the last 6 months

### Your Task

1. **Classify each group** (misinformation, disinformation, or uncertain)
2. **Assign confidence levels** for each classification
3. **Identify what additional information** would change your confidence
4. **Recommend appropriate interventions** for each group

### Constraints

- You have 15 minutes
- You have access only to public data (posts, account histories, timing)
- You cannot contact the original poster or verify their intent directly

### Worked Solution

**Group A (Alternative health accounts)**:
- Classification: Likely misinformation (medium confidence)
- Reasoning: Consistent with their existing worldview, sharing pattern suggests genuine belief, emotional engagement in comments suggests personal investment rather than strategic deployment
- What would change confidence: Evidence of coordination between these accounts, financial incentives, or prior history of knowingly false claims would shift toward disinformation assessment
- Intervention: Gen 3 prebunking for audiences who follow these accounts; Gen 2 debunking may work for some followers but risks backfire if delivered confrontationally

**Group B (Concerned personal accounts)**:
- Classification: Misinformation (high confidence)
- Reasoning: Sharing pattern shows genuine uncertainty ("Should I ask my doctor?"), no coordination signals, diverse account histories suggest organic spread
- What would change confidence: Almost nothing—this pattern is characteristic of misinformation spread by concerned individuals
- Intervention: Gen 2 debunking with empathetic framing acknowledging legitimate health concerns; provide accurate information about what the study actually showed

**Group C (Coordinated cluster)**:
- Classification: Uncertain, leaning toward disinformation (medium confidence)
- Reasoning: Near-simultaneous posting of identical text by recently created accounts is a coordination signal. However, this could be coordinated ideological activism rather than deceptive intent.
- What would change confidence: Account creation patterns, whether accounts show other coordinated behaviour, connections to known inauthentic networks
- Intervention: Gen 4 moderation referral for platform investigation of coordinated inauthentic behaviour; regardless of intent, the behaviour pattern may violate platform policies

**Overall assessment**: The same content is moving through different channels with different dynamics. Intervention should be tailored to each, not a one-size-fits-all response.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming all spreaders have identical intent**: The three groups require different assessments and interventions
2. **Treating coordination as proof of disinformation**: Coordination signals suggest investigation, not automatic disinformation classification
3. **Ignoring the partial truth**: The content is not entirely fabricated—it exaggerates real research. This complicates both classification and response.
4. **Overclaiming on original poster**: A history of alternative health content does not prove intent to deceive; it may reflect genuine belief in alternative health frameworks
5. **Underweighting harm**: Regardless of classification, if people stop taking prescribed medication, harm can occur. Response urgency should reflect potential harm, not just classification certainty.

## Practice Exercise 2: Leaked Document Assessment

### Scenario

Anonymous documents are released online alleging financial misconduct by a senior government official. The documents appear to show bank transfers to offshore accounts and communications discussing how to hide the transactions.

Initial observations:
- Documents are high quality PDFs with official-looking letterheads
- Released through an encrypted messaging channel by an anonymous account
- A journalist has contacted you asking whether they should report on the documents
- The accused official has denied the allegations and claimed the documents are fabricated
- You cannot independently verify the authenticity of the documents

### Your Task

Assess whether this is:
- **Legitimate whistleblowing**: Authentic documents exposing genuine misconduct
- **Malinformation**: Authentic documents but released in a manipulative context or timing
- **Disinformation**: Fabricated documents designed to harm the official's reputation

### Constraints

- You have 30 minutes
- You cannot verify document authenticity with certainty
- The official's denial is expected regardless of whether documents are real or fake
- Publication could cause significant harm if documents are fabricated
- Non-publication could protect genuine corruption if documents are authentic

### Worked Solution

**Step 1: Assess what can be verified**

Without authenticating the documents themselves, examine:
- Source behaviour: Has the anonymous source provided any verifiable claims that can be checked?
- Document metadata: Are there forensic indicators of fabrication or authenticity?
- Contextual plausibility: Do the alleged transactions align with known timelines and financial systems?
- Pattern matching: Does this resemble previous authenticated leaks or previous known fabrication operations?

**Step 2: Map the confidence levels**

For each possibility, assess likelihood:

- Legitimate whistleblowing: Plausible if source behaviour is consistent with genuine leakers (willing to provide additional context, has coherent explanation for access)
- Malinformation: Consider timing—does release coincide with electoral or political moment that maximises damage? Could authentic documents be selectively released to mislead?
- Disinformation: What would be required to fabricate these documents? Do they require insider knowledge or could they be created externally? Are there subtle errors inconsistent with genuine documents?

**Step 3: Decision framework**

The journalist's question requires guidance on responsible reporting:

If **strong authenticity signals**: Report with appropriate caveats about source and verification status
If **strong fabrication signals**: Do not report as news; may report on the apparent fabrication attempt
If **genuinely uncertain**: Consider partial reporting ("Documents have emerged claiming X; their authenticity cannot be verified; official denies allegations") or delay for further verification

**Step 4: What not to do**

- Do not treat the official's denial as evidence of authenticity (they would deny regardless)
- Do not treat the official's denial as evidence of fabrication (guilty parties also deny)
- Do not assume anonymous = malicious (many legitimate whistleblowers require anonymity)
- Do not assume anonymous = trustworthy (anonymity also enables fabrication)

**Assessment output**: In this scenario with limited information, responsible classification is "uncertain—requires additional verification before high-confidence assessment." This is not failure; it is appropriate epistemic humility.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Treating authenticity as binary**: Documents can be partially authentic (real documents with fabricated additions), selectively authentic (real but missing context), or entirely fabricated
2. **Rushing to classification**: Pressure to "make a call" should not override appropriate uncertainty
3. **Conflating authenticity with public interest**: Even authentic documents may not warrant publication depending on context and harm
4. **Ignoring operational patterns**: Prior fabrication campaigns in the region/context are relevant evidence
5. **Assuming verification is impossible**: Partial verification is often possible even when full verification is not—check what can be checked

## Regional Focus: EU Definition and Framework

### Context: How the EU Defines FIMI

The European Union has developed the concept of Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference (FIMI) as its primary framework for understanding information threats. FIMI is defined as a pattern of behaviour that threatens or has the potential to negatively impact values, procedures, and political processes. Key characteristics:

- **Foreign or domestic**: While "foreign" is in the name, the framework acknowledges domestic actors may employ similar tactics
- **Manipulative**: Intentional, coordinated, deceptive
- **Pattern-based**: Focuses on behaviour patterns rather than individual pieces of content
- **Impact-oriented**: Concerned with actual or potential harm to democratic processes

**Comparison with national approaches**: Some member states focus more on "disinformation" (content-based) while FIMI emphasises "manipulation" (behaviour-based). This distinction affects what gets measured and how responses are designed.

### The Digital Services Act (DSA) Framework

The DSA creates obligations for platforms operating in the EU regarding disinformation:

**Transparency requirements**:
- Platforms must publish transparency reports on content moderation
- Advertising transparency including who paid and targeting criteria
- Algorithmic transparency for very large platforms

**Risk assessment obligations**:
- Very large platforms must assess systemic risks including disinformation
- Must implement mitigation measures
- Independent auditing of compliance

**Crisis response protocols**:
- Commission can require platforms to assess and mitigate specific disinformation threats during crises
- Coordinated response mechanisms for cross-border information threats

**Practical implications for practitioners**:
- Increased access to data through researcher data access provisions
- More consistent platform policies across EU member states
- Formal channels for flagging concerns to regulators

### Country Variations

EU member states have adopted different legal approaches:

**France**: Has specific laws against election-period disinformation, with provisions for rapid judicial takedown of false information during electoral campaigns

**Germany**: The Network Enforcement Act (NetzDG) requires platforms to remove "unlawful content" including certain false claims, with significant fines for non-compliance

**Baltic States**: Have developed specific frameworks for countering foreign information operations, with dedicated government units and civil society coordination

**Nordic countries**: Generally emphasise media literacy and resilience-building over legal restrictions on content

**Common tensions**:
- Balancing disinformation response with freedom of expression
- Defining government role versus platform responsibility versus civil society action
- Handling cross-border content with different national standards

### Practical Implications for Practitioners

Working within the EU context means:

1. **Know your jurisdiction**: Legal frameworks vary significantly; what is possible in one member state may not be in another
2. **Understand platform obligations**: The DSA creates specific requirements that affect how platforms respond to flagged content
3. **Access new data sources**: Researcher data access provisions create opportunities for evidence-based analysis
4. **Coordinate across borders**: Many information operations are cross-border; response increasingly requires international coordination
5. **Balance frameworks**: FIMI is one framework among many; academic, journalistic, and civil society frameworks may emphasise different aspects

## Expert Perspective: The Classification Debate

### Research Insights: Is Intent-Based Classification Useful?

Academic debate continues on whether distinguishing dis- from misinformation based on intent serves analytical or practical purposes.

**Arguments for intent-based distinction**:
- Different interventions are appropriate for different intents
- Understanding actor motivation helps predict future behaviour
- Attribution supports accountability mechanisms
- Legal frameworks often require intent determination

**Arguments against emphasis on intent**:
- Intent is often unprovable without insider access
- Harm occurs regardless of intent
- Focus on intent can delay response
- Creates false binary when intent is often mixed or ambiguous

**Current research consensus** (medium confidence): Intent-based classification is analytically useful but should not be prerequisite for response. Harm and impact can be assessed independently of intent and often should drive intervention timing.

### Practitioner View: Why Focus on Harm Over Intent

Operational analysts often prioritise differently than academic frameworks:

**Harm-first approach**:
- What damage is this content causing or likely to cause?
- How quickly is harm accumulating?
- What interventions can reduce harm regardless of intent classification?

**Practical reasoning**:
- Harm can be observed; intent must be inferred
- Intervention timing often matters more than classification precision
- Resources are limited; proving intent is expensive

**When intent matters operationally**:
- When choosing between debunking (works better for misinformation) and moderation (may be necessary for disinformation)
- When attribution is required for accountability
- When legal action is contemplated

### Open Questions

**Can we ever prove intent?**
Without access to internal communications or confessions, intent must be inferred from behaviour. This inference can be strong or weak but is rarely definitive. Practitioners must become comfortable with probabilistic rather than certain assessments.

**Does proving intent matter?**
For some purposes (legal accountability, sanctions), yes. For others (harm reduction, audience protection), less so. The question "does it matter" depends on what you are trying to accomplish.

**How do we handle mixed cases?**
Most real cases involve mixed motivations, multiple actors with different intents, and content that evolved as it spread. Binary classification often fails to capture this complexity. Confidence levels and actor-specific assessment help but do not fully resolve the challenge.

## Historical Context: From Propaganda to Disinformation

### Origins: Cold War "Dezinformatsiya"

The term "disinformation" entered English from Russian "dezinformatsiya," referring to Soviet intelligence practices of spreading false information to deceive adversaries. Cold War disinformation was characterised by:

- **State-directed**: Operations run by intelligence services
- **Resource-intensive**: Required media outlets, agents, forged documents
- **Slow-moving**: Took months or years to seed and develop narratives
- **Targeted audiences**: Often aimed at specific decision-makers or narrow publics

Classic examples include forged documents alleging NATO plans and fabricated stories about US government actions, often surfaced through intermediary publications to obscure origin.

### Evolution: From State Propaganda to Social Media Era

The information environment has transformed substantially:

**Pre-internet era**:
- Information gatekeepers (editors, producers) controlled what reached audiences
- Disinformation required access to these gatekeepers or creation of alternative channels
- Verification, while not perfect, had structural advantages

**Social media era**:
- Gatekeeping distributed or eliminated
- Anyone can reach large audiences without editorial review
- Speed of spread accelerates dramatically
- Algorithmic curation replaces editorial judgement
- Verification challenges multiply

**Hybrid warfare era**:
- State actors adapt to new environment
- Non-state actors gain capabilities previously reserved for states
- Commercial disinformation-for-hire services emerge
- Domestic and foreign actors use similar tactics

### Current State: Democratisation of Capabilities

The tools and techniques of disinformation are now widely accessible:

**Low barriers to entry**:
- Creating social media accounts is free
- Basic manipulation techniques are widely documented
- Coordination can be achieved through ideological networks without formal direction

**Professionalization**:
- Commercial services offer disinformation campaigns for hire
- Influencer networks can be rented for amplification
- Fake engagement (likes, shares, comments) is purchasable

**Sophistication spectrum**:
- Low sophistication: Individual actors spreading false claims
- Medium sophistication: Coordinated networks, cross-platform strategies
- High sophistication: State-backed operations with substantial resources

### Future Trajectory: AI-Generated Content Challenges

Emerging technologies create new challenges:

**Generative AI capabilities**:
- Text generation enables high-volume content creation
- Image generation enables fabricated visual "evidence"
- Audio and video synthesis (deepfakes) challenge authentication
- Personalisation at scale becomes possible

**What changes**:
- Volume: More content can be generated faster
- Quality: Synthetic content becomes harder to distinguish from authentic
- Attribution: Technical attribution becomes more difficult

**What stays the same**:
- Fundamental dynamics of belief and spread
- Importance of trust networks and source credibility
- Need for media literacy and critical thinking

**Appropriate response framing**: AI changes the production of disinformation more than its fundamental dynamics. Focus on building resilience and verification capabilities rather than assuming AI makes the problem unsolvable.

## Quick Reference: Classification Checklist

### Rapid Classification Checklist

**Step 1: Verify falsity**
- [ ] Is the core claim false or misleading?
- [ ] Have you checked against reliable sources?
- [ ] If accurate, stop—this is not a dis/mis/mal-information problem

**Step 2: Assess intent signals**
- [ ] Check account history—pattern of false claims?
- [ ] Check production quality—amateur or professional?
- [ ] Check coordination—others posting identical content?
- [ ] Check timing—aligned with events that maximise harm?

**Step 3: Rate signal strength**
- Strong signals present (2+ of: coordination, professional production, history of false claims, harm-maximising timing)
- Medium signals present (some indicators, could be genuine error)
- Weak signals only (content is false, but limited intent evidence)

**Step 4: Classify with confidence**
- Disinformation (high/medium/low confidence)
- Misinformation (high/medium/low confidence)
- Malinformation (true content, harmful context)
- Uncertain—requires additional investigation

**Step 5: Check for malinformation indicators**
- [ ] Is accurate information presented out of context?
- [ ] Is private information exposed to cause harm?
- [ ] Is timing designed to maximise damage from true information?

### Decision Framework (Text Description)

Start: Is content false or misleading?
- No → Not dis/mis/mal-information. End.
- Yes → Continue

Are there strong intent signals?
- Yes → Likely disinformation. Assess confidence and proceed to intervention selection.
- No → Continue

Are there medium intent signals?
- Yes → Investigate further if time permits; otherwise classify as uncertain/possible disinformation with low confidence
- No → Continue

Is the spreader profile consistent with genuine error?
- Yes → Likely misinformation. Assess confidence.
- No → Uncertain. Note ambiguity.

Final check: Is accurate information being weaponised?
- Yes → Consider malinformation classification
- No → Maintain current classification

Output: Classification + confidence level + recommended next action

### Escalation Criteria

Escalate for additional review or resources when:

**Harm indicators**:
- Content relates to imminent physical safety (health, violence)
- Content targets electoral integrity near election
- Content involves vulnerable populations (children, elderly)
- Offline consequences observed (harassment, violence)

**Confidence indicators**:
- Classification is uncertain after initial assessment
- Attribution would change response options
- Legal or policy action is contemplated

**Scale indicators**:
- Rapid spread suggests coordinated amplification
- Cross-platform coordination observed
- Resources exceed individual analyst capacity

**Sensitivity indicators**:
- High-profile targets involved
- State actor attribution is possible
- Response could have diplomatic implications

## Stop Rule

Stop classification when:
- **Accuracy confirmed**: Content is true—move to different analysis
- **Intent unprovable**: You can assess harm without proving intent
- **Intervention doesn't require classification**: Sometimes you respond to harm, not category

Don't spend hours proving intent when harm assessment is sufficient for action.

---

## DIM Mapping

Classification determines intervention type:

**Misinformation** (honest error):
- **Gen 2** (Debunking): Correction works because sharer didn't intend to deceive
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): Prevent future errors through inoculation

**Disinformation** (deliberate):
- **Gen 4** (Moderation): May require platform action
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Design changes to reduce amplification
- Gen 2 alone often fails—correcting someone who knows they're lying

**Malinformation** (true but harmful):
- Context restoration, not fact-checking
- Often requires different legal/ethical framework

---

## Key Takeaway

The dis/mis/mal distinction matters for intervention choice, not moral judgement. You can't always prove intent, and you don't always need to. Focus on: Is it false? Is it harmful? What response fits?
