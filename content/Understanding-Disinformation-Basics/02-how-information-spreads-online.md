## Purpose

You'll learn why some content spreads and other content doesn't—so you can assess whether viral spread indicates manipulation or effective content.

---

## Scenario

A piece of content goes from 100 shares to 50,000 in 6 hours. Your team asks: "Is this organic or coordinated?" You have:

- **Time available**: 45 minutes for initial assessment
- **Stakes**: Determines whether to flag for investigation or accept as organic virality
- **Data access**: Public engagement metrics, posting timestamps, limited account data
- **Constraint**: Virality alone proves nothing

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What spread pattern are you seeing?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High that this is organic vs coordinated
3. **Next action**: Investigate further, monitor, or close?

---

## Method: Spread Analysis Workflow

### Start here: Establish baseline

Before assuming manipulation:
- Is there a real-world trigger? (News event, celebrity post, controversy)
- Does the content have inherent viral properties? (Emotional, shareable, identity-reinforcing)
- Is this spread unusual for this topic/account?

**If clear trigger exists** → Organic spread is plausible
**If no trigger and sudden spike** → Worth investigating

### Step 2: Map the spread pattern

Examine how content moved:
- Did it spread through a few large accounts (broadcast pattern)?
- Did it spread peer-to-peer through many small accounts (network pattern)?
- Did multiple unconnected accounts post simultaneously (coordination signal)?

**Organic patterns**: Gradual build, clear origin point, natural network spread
**Suspicious patterns**: Sudden spikes without trigger, simultaneous posting, identical content across unconnected accounts

### Step 3: Check amplification mechanisms

Content spreads through:
- **Algorithmic amplification**: Platforms promote engaging content
- **Network effects**: People share what their friends share
- **Coordinated amplification**: Organised efforts to boost content

The same outcome (viral spread) can have different causes.

---

## How Platforms Shape Spread

**Algorithms optimise for engagement**, not accuracy:
- Content triggering strong emotions gets promoted
- Controversial content generates comments → more visibility
- Time-on-platform is the metric, not truth

**Network structure matters**:
- Highly connected accounts can broadcast to millions
- Clustered communities can create rapid internal spread
- Bridge accounts connect otherwise separate groups

**Timing matters**:
- First-mover advantage in breaking news
- Platform activity peaks affect visibility
- Coordinated timing can game trending algorithms

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (coordination likely):
- Simultaneous posting by unconnected accounts (within seconds/minutes)
- Identical content with no clear original source
- Spread pattern inconsistent with account sizes/networks
- Accounts activated specifically for this content

**Medium signals** (worth investigating):
- Unusually rapid spread for this type of content
- Engagement patterns don't match historical norms
- Mix of organic and suspicious accounts sharing

**Weak signals** (insufficient alone):
- Content went viral (viral happens organically too)
- Content is emotional (emotions drive organic sharing too)
- You don't like the content (bias isn't evidence)

## Case Study 1: The Viral Vaccine Misinformation Video (2021)

### Background

In May 2021, a 26-minute documentary-style video titled "Plandemic" accumulated millions of views across social media platforms within days. The video featured a discredited researcher making false claims about COVID-19, vaccines, and public health institutions. This case offers lessons about distinguishing coordinated amplification from organic virality.

### The Spread Pattern: What the Data Showed

**Initial release (Days 1-2)**:
- Video first appeared on a relatively small YouTube channel
- Within hours, clips began appearing across Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram
- Early shares came primarily from health skeptic communities and conspiracy-oriented groups
- Engagement metrics showed unusually high share-to-view ratios

**Acceleration phase (Days 3-5)**:
- Multiple Facebook groups with overlapping membership shared the video near-simultaneously
- Some coordination signals present: identical captions, similar posting times across groups
- However, much of the spread showed characteristics of genuine viral content
- The video had inherent viral properties: emotional appeals, narrative structure, claims of suppressed truth

**Platform response (Days 5-10)**:
- YouTube removed the original video for violating medical misinformation policies
- Facebook and Twitter began removing copies
- Removals triggered secondary spread: "watch before it gets deleted" framing
- Content migrated to alternative platforms with less moderation

### Analysis: Organic, Coordinated, or Both?

**Confidence assessment**: Medium confidence that initial spread involved some coordination, high confidence that subsequent spread was primarily organic.

**Evidence for coordination element**:
- The video's professional production quality exceeded typical user-generated content
- Some early sharing accounts showed patterns consistent with organised promotion
- Cross-platform posting timing in initial hours suggested planning
- The video's release coincided with public health announcements (timing optimisation)

**Evidence for organic element**:
- Content genuinely resonated with audiences already skeptical of institutions
- Once seeded, spread followed natural network patterns
- Share patterns matched genuine viral content (emotional engagement, identity reinforcement)
- Many sharers were authentic accounts with long histories, sharing because they believed the claims

**Key insight**: This case illustrates that coordination and organic spread are not mutually exclusive. Content can be strategically seeded by a coordinated group and then spread organically because it resonates with real audiences. The correct assessment is often "both/and" rather than "either/or."

### Platform Response Assessment

**What platforms did**:
- Content removal after several days
- Some labelling of misinformation before removal
- Reduced algorithmic promotion of related content

**Effectiveness assessment** (medium confidence):
- Removal reduced total reach but did not stop spread
- Late timing meant millions had already viewed the content
- Removal framing ("censorship") became part of the narrative, potentially increasing appeal among some audiences
- Migration to alternative platforms reduced platform visibility but concentrated engaged audiences

**What this suggests**:
- Speed matters: earlier intervention would have reduced total exposure
- Removal alone is insufficient: counter-narratives and prebunking needed
- Platform policies created predictable responses that spreaders anticipated

### Lessons for Spread Analysis

1. **Viral properties can coexist with coordination**: Don't dismiss coordination signals because content has genuine appeal
2. **The same content serves different functions for different sharers**: Coordinated initial spread, organic subsequent spread
3. **Platform responses become part of the spread dynamic**: Anticipate how removal or labelling will affect subsequent patterns
4. **Attribution confidence should be specific**: "Some coordination in initial seeding" is different from "coordinated disinformation campaign"
5. **Harm occurs regardless of spread mechanism**: Whether organic or coordinated, the health misinformation caused damage

## Case Study 2: GameStop Stock Surge (2021)

### Background

In January 2021, shares of the video game retailer GameStop rose from approximately $20 to nearly $500 in a matter of weeks, driven largely by retail investors coordinating on the Reddit forum r/WallStreetBets. This case demonstrates what authentic network mobilisation looks like—providing a crucial comparison point for analysts trying to distinguish organic from manipulated spread.

### The Spread Pattern: Authentic Network Mobilisation

**Community formation (2020)**:
- Users on r/WallStreetBets had been discussing GameStop for months
- Analysis posts explained the "short squeeze" thesis in detail
- Community norms developed organically: shared vocabulary, in-jokes, identity markers
- No evidence of external direction or hidden coordination

**Mobilisation phase (January 2021)**:
- As the stock rose, posts gained visibility within and beyond the subreddit
- Media coverage accelerated spread to broader audiences
- New participants joined, some understanding the thesis, others following momentum
- Spread pattern showed classic viral characteristics with clear origin community

**Institutional response**:
- Trading platforms restricted purchases of GameStop shares
- Restrictions triggered massive backlash and further attention
- Political figures across the spectrum commented, amplifying further
- Regulatory scrutiny followed

### Analysis: What Makes This Organic?

**Confidence assessment**: High confidence this was organic network mobilisation, not coordinated manipulation.

**Strong organic signals**:
- Transparent coordination: All discussion was public, on a public forum
- Long formation period: Community and thesis developed over months, not manufactured overnight
- Genuine participant investment: Users were risking their own money based on their analysis
- Diverse participation: Users joined for varied reasons (profit, ideology, entertainment)
- Emergent leadership: Influential posters gained status through contribution quality, not assignment

**Contrast with coordinated campaigns**:
- Coordinated campaigns typically involve hidden direction; this was openly coordinated
- Manufactured campaigns use inauthentic accounts; these were predominantly real people
- Astroturfing mimics organic activity; this was organic activity
- Coordinated campaigns often have external beneficiaries; participants were the beneficiaries

**Why the distinction matters**:
- "Coordination" in the sense of people openly agreeing to act together is not the same as "coordinated inauthentic behaviour"
- Organic mobilisation is a feature of healthy public discourse, not a threat to it
- Mischaracterising authentic collective action as manipulation undermines legitimate organising

### Comparison: Organic Mobilisation vs Coordinated Manipulation

| Dimension | GameStop (Organic) | Typical Manipulation Campaign |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| Transparency | Fully public | Hidden coordination |
| Account authenticity | Real people, verified histories | Inauthentic or compromised accounts |
| Participant benefit | Participants benefit directly | Benefits flow to hidden actors |
| Formation timeline | Months of organic development | Rapid deployment |
| Content variation | High variation, individual voices | Repetitive, templated |
| Response to scrutiny | Defiant transparency | Obfuscation, deletion |

### Lessons for Disinformation Analysis

1. **Open coordination is not manipulation**: People agreeing publicly to act together is different from hidden influence operations
2. **Authenticity leaves traces**: Real communities have histories, variation, and organic development
3. **Analyst bias is a risk**: If you dislike an outcome, you may be tempted to find manipulation where none exists
4. **The "who benefits" question has limits**: In organic mobilisation, participants benefit; this does not make it inauthentic
5. **Scale alone is not evidence**: Large-scale collective action can be entirely organic

### Why This Case Matters for Your Work

When analysing suspected manipulation, you need comparison cases of authentic virality and genuine mobilisation. The GameStop case provides a benchmark:

- If a movement has similar characteristics (transparency, authentic accounts, organic formation, participant benefit), your coordination hypothesis should have low confidence
- If a movement lacks these characteristics (hidden direction, inauthentic accounts, rapid deployment, external beneficiaries), your coordination hypothesis gains support

The goal is accurate assessment, not finding manipulation in every large-scale online phenomenon.

## Tool Tutorial: Basic Spread Analysis

### What You Can Learn from Public Data

Before accessing specialised tools, understand what publicly available information can tell you about content spread. This tutorial covers techniques accessible to analysts without platform-level data access.

### Timestamp Analysis

**What it reveals**: The timing of posts can indicate whether spread is organic or coordinated.

**Basic method**:
1. Collect timestamps of earliest posts containing the content
2. Convert all timestamps to the same timezone (UTC recommended)
3. Calculate time differences between posts
4. Look for clusters and patterns

**Interpretation guide**:

- Posts spread over hours/days with clear origin point: consistent with organic spread
- Multiple posts within seconds/minutes across unconnected accounts: coordination signal (medium strength)
- Posts clustered at unusual hours for claimed locations: worth investigating
- Steady increase over time: typical viral pattern
- Sudden spike with no preceding activity: worth investigating

**Limitations**:
- Platform APIs may not provide exact timestamps
- You may see a sample of posts, not all posts
- Time clustering can have innocent explanations (news events cause simultaneous reactions)
- Sophisticated actors know to stagger posting times

### Account History Analysis

**What to examine**:
- Account creation date relative to content posting
- Posting history and topics
- Follower/following patterns
- Profile completeness

**Medium signals of inauthenticity**:
- Account created shortly before posting the content
- Sudden topic shift (account that posted about cooking now posting political content)
- Unusual follower/following ratios
- Generic or stock profile images
- Posting patterns inconsistent with human behaviour (24/7 activity, identical intervals)

**Important caveats**:
- New accounts can be real people
- Topic shifts happen for legitimate reasons (major events cause people to post outside their usual topics)
- These signals require pattern analysis across multiple accounts, not individual assessment
- Cultural differences affect "normal" platform behaviour

### Network Mapping (Basic Level)

**What you can observe without special tools**:
- Who shared from whom (when visible)
- Common followers between accounts sharing content
- Cross-platform posting by same accounts
- Reply and mention patterns

**Simple network questions**:
- Is there a clear origin point, or did content appear simultaneously in multiple places?
- Are the sharing accounts connected to each other?
- Do accounts sharing this content also share other similar content?
- Are there "bridge" accounts connecting otherwise separate communities?

**Tool options** (varying access levels):
- Manual observation and documentation (accessible to all)
- Social Bearing, Followerwonk for Twitter analysis (public tools with limitations)
- CrowdTangle for Facebook/Instagram (requires application, researchers and journalists)
- Academic research tools (institutional access required)

### What CrowdTangle-Style Data Tells You (And Doesn't)

CrowdTangle (and similar tools) provides visibility into how content spreads across Facebook, Instagram, and Reddit. Understanding its capabilities and limitations is essential.

**What it shows**:
- Public posts containing specific URLs, keywords, or from specific pages/groups
- Engagement metrics over time (likes, shares, comments)
- Which pages/groups shared content and when
- Geographic and demographic patterns (limited)

**What it doesn't show**:
- Private groups and messages
- Individual user shares (pages and groups only)
- Algorithmic promotion decisions
- Why content spread (only that it did)
- Activity on platforms it doesn't cover

**Interpretation caution**:
- CrowdTangle shows public activity; much sharing happens in private spaces
- Absence from CrowdTangle does not mean content didn't spread
- The tool shows correlation (content appeared here, then there) not causation
- Platform changes affect data availability over time

### Documentation Best Practices

When conducting spread analysis, document systematically:

**Record for each post**:
- Platform and URL (URLs may be removed; archive when possible)
- Timestamp (with timezone)
- Account information (username, follower count, creation date)
- Content (exact text, media description)
- Engagement metrics at time of observation
- Your observation timestamp

**Record your analysis**:
- Hypothesis at each stage
- Confidence level and reasoning
- What evidence would change your assessment
- Limitations of your data access

**Why documentation matters**:
- Content and accounts may be removed
- You may need to revisit analysis with new information
- Others may need to verify or build on your work
- Your reasoning should be transparent and challengeable

## Practice Exercise 1: Coordinated vs Organic

### Scenario

You are analysing the spread of a video claiming that a new municipal policy will "destroy local businesses." The video appeared 3 days ago and has accumulated significant engagement. You have the following data:

**Initial spread (first 6 hours)**:
- 08:14 UTC: First post by Account A (local business owner, 2,300 followers, account created 2018, regular posting about local issues)
- 08:47 UTC: Shared by Account B (community group page, 12,000 followers, established local group)
- 09:02 UTC: Shared by Account C (account created 2 weeks ago, 47 followers, profile photo is stock image)
- 09:03 UTC: Shared by Account D (account created 3 weeks ago, 89 followers, profile photo is stock image)
- 09:04 UTC: Shared by Account E (account created 2 weeks ago, 52 followers, no profile photo)
- 09:31 UTC: Shared by Account F (local politician, 8,500 followers, verified account)
- 10:15 UTC: Shared by Account G (local news outlet, 45,000 followers)

**Subsequent spread (hours 6-72)**:
- Hundreds of shares from diverse local accounts
- Video picked up by regional news
- Engagement includes substantive comments debating the policy

**Content analysis**:
- Video is emotionally compelling, features affected business owners
- Some claims are exaggerated but core concern appears genuine
- Production quality is semi-professional (not amateur phone video, not broadcast quality)

### Your Task

**Step 1**: Assess the initial spread pattern

Look at the first 6 hours of data. What do you observe about:
- The timing of posts?
- The account characteristics?
- Any patterns that stand out?

**Step 2**: Classify the accounts

For each account type, assess whether the sharing pattern is consistent with organic behaviour:
- Account A (original poster)
- Account B (community group)
- Accounts C, D, E (cluster)
- Account F (politician)
- Account G (news outlet)

**Step 3**: Form your hypothesis

Based on the data, what is your assessment?
- Organic spread
- Coordinated amplification
- Mixed (some coordination, mostly organic)
- Insufficient data

What is your confidence level? What would change it?

### Worked Solution

**Step 1 analysis**:

The timing data reveals a notable pattern:
- Accounts C, D, and E posted within a 2-minute window (09:02-09:04)
- All three accounts were created within the past 3 weeks
- All three have characteristics associated with inauthentic accounts (low followers, stock/missing photos)

This represents a medium signal of coordination. Three near-simultaneous posts from recently-created accounts with similar characteristics is worth noting.

However, the broader pattern shows organic characteristics:
- Clear origin point (Account A, a genuine local business owner)
- Natural escalation through community networks (B), then political amplification (F), then media coverage (G)
- Subsequent spread involved diverse, established accounts with substantive engagement

**Step 2 classification**:

- Account A: High confidence authentic (established account, posting history consistent, genuine local connection)
- Account B: High confidence authentic (established community group, consistent with normal sharing)
- Accounts C, D, E: Medium confidence inauthentic (new accounts, suspicious characteristics, coordinated timing)
- Account F: High confidence authentic (verified politician, sharing locally-relevant content is expected)
- Account G: High confidence authentic (news outlet covering local story)

**Step 3 hypothesis**:

**Assessment**: Mixed pattern—possible small-scale amplification attempt within predominantly organic spread.

**Confidence**: Medium

**Reasoning**:
- The C/D/E cluster shows coordination signals, but represents a small fraction of total spread
- Removing C/D/E from the analysis, the spread pattern remains organic
- The video had genuine viral properties (emotional, locally relevant, real concerns)
- Subsequent spread was clearly organic (diverse accounts, substantive engagement)

**What would change confidence**:
- If C/D/E accounts showed connections to known inauthentic networks: increase confidence in coordination
- If similar clusters appeared in other communities around similar content: increase confidence in broader campaign
- If C/D/E accounts had longer histories with consistent local engagement: decrease confidence in coordination (they might be real but new to this platform)

**Recommended action**: Document the C/D/E cluster for pattern matching with future incidents. Do not characterise the overall spread as "coordinated"—the accurate description is "organic spread with possible minor amplification attempt that did not significantly affect overall trajectory."

### Common Mistakes

1. **Overclaiming from limited signals**: The C/D/E cluster is notable but does not mean the entire spread was coordinated
2. **Ignoring organic elements**: The video had genuine appeal; assuming all spread was artificial ignores evidence
3. **Conflating suspicious accounts with proven inauthenticity**: C/D/E show signals worth investigating, not proof of coordination
4. **Missing the forest for the trees**: Three suspicious accounts in a spread reaching hundreds of genuine local accounts is a minor element, not the main story
5. **Treating coordination as binary**: "Some coordination signals in initial hours, predominantly organic overall" is more accurate than forced binary classification

## Practice Exercise 2: Breaking News Spread Assessment

### Scenario

You receive an alert at 14:30 UTC about a viral claim. A post alleges that a chemical spill has occurred at a local factory, with a video showing smoke rising from an industrial facility. The post is spreading rapidly and people are expressing concern about evacuations.

**Data available at 14:30 (you have 20 minutes to assess)**:

- Original post appeared at 13:45 UTC from a local resident's account (8 years old, 340 followers, regular posting about local topics)
- Video shows smoke/steam rising from a facility; the factory's name is visible
- Post text claims "major chemical leak, authorities not telling us anything"
- 2,400 shares in 45 minutes
- Local emergency services have not issued any statements
- Local news outlets have not reported on it
- Weather data shows it's a cold day (relevant for distinguishing steam from smoke)

**Additional posts appearing**:
- Several local accounts sharing with concerned comments
- Two accounts with generic names sharing identical "GET OUT NOW" messages (created in past month)
- A local official's account posted "We're looking into reports" at 14:15

### Your Task

**Immediate assessment questions**:
1. Is the core claim (chemical spill) verified, unverified, or falsified?
2. What is the spread pattern telling you?
3. What are the potential harms if you wait vs act?
4. What additional information would you seek?
5. When should you stop investigating and make a call?

### Working with Incomplete Data

This exercise deliberately provides incomplete information because real-time assessment rarely has complete data.

**What you know**:
- There is a video of something (smoke or steam) at the facility
- A local resident with an established account posted it first
- Spread is rapid but not showing strong coordination signals
- Emergency services haven't confirmed or denied
- Some suspicious accounts are amplifying with alarmist messages

**What you don't know**:
- Whether a chemical spill actually occurred
- Whether the video is current or old
- Whether the smoke/steam is from normal operations or an incident
- Whether the suspicious accounts are connected to a campaign or are opportunistic

**Decision framework for incomplete data**:

1. **Assess verifiable elements first**:
   - Can you verify the video location matches the claimed factory? (Check against satellite imagery, street view)
   - Can you verify the video is recent? (Weather conditions, lighting, shadows)
   - Can you contact the facility or local authorities directly?

2. **Assess harm scenarios**:
   - If real and you dismiss it: People may not evacuate when they should
   - If false and you amplify concern: Unnecessary panic, potential injuries from rushed evacuation
   - If uncertain and you wait: More information may emerge, but delay may be costly in either direction

3. **Consider the source pattern**:
   - Original post from established local account: increases credibility of genuine local concern
   - Spread through local networks: consistent with real local event
   - Suspicious "GET OUT NOW" accounts: worth noting but don't determine whether the event is real

### When to Stop Investigating

In real-time scenarios, you cannot investigate indefinitely. Set clear stopping points:

**Stop and escalate when**:
- You've verified the claim is true (actual emergency)
- You've found evidence the claim is false (e.g., video is from different location/time)
- The 20-minute window is reached and you have enough for a preliminary assessment
- Official sources provide confirmation or denial

**Stop and monitor when**:
- Evidence is genuinely mixed
- Official response is pending
- Harm potential is moderate but not immediate

**Do not stop prematurely because**:
- You have a hypothesis (test it)
- The claim seems implausible (implausible things happen)
- You want closure (uncertainty is sometimes the honest assessment)

### Worked Solution

**Assessment at 14:50 (20 minutes elapsed)**:

**Core claim status**: Unverified. No confirmation or denial from authorities. Video shows something at the facility but nature unclear.

**Spread pattern**: Predominantly organic with minor suspicious amplification. Original poster is authentic. Spread matches local concern pattern. Two suspicious accounts noted but not driving spread.

**Harm assessment**:
- Potential harm if real and dismissed: High (public safety)
- Potential harm if false and amplified: Medium (panic, but people will learn truth relatively quickly)
- Asymmetry suggests erring toward caution on public safety while avoiding alarmist framing

**Recommended action**:
1. Do not characterise this as "disinformation" without evidence the claim is false
2. Do not amplify the "GET OUT NOW" panic framing
3. Note the unverified status and point to official channels for updates
4. Continue monitoring for official statements
5. Document the suspicious accounts for pattern analysis

**Key insight**: The spread analysis question (organic vs coordinated) is secondary to the verification question (is there actually a chemical spill). Not everything that spreads fast is disinformation—sometimes it's genuine local concern about a real event.

**What happened next (for learning purposes)**: The factory released a statement 45 minutes later confirming that a steam release valve had malfunctioned, creating visible steam but posing no chemical hazard. The original poster's concern was genuine if ultimately unfounded. The suspicious "GET OUT NOW" accounts appeared to be opportunistic (posting alarmist content to gain followers) rather than part of a coordinated disinformation campaign.

## Regional Focus: Platform Differences in EU

### The European Platform Landscape

Information spread patterns vary significantly across European platforms and language communities. Understanding these differences is essential for accurate analysis.

### Platform Usage Patterns

**Major platform variations**:

Western Europe tends toward global platforms (Facebook, Instagram, Twitter/X, TikTok) but with significant national variation. Eastern Europe has higher penetration of local and Russian-language platforms in some countries.

**Country-specific patterns** (examples, not exhaustive):

- **Germany**: Strong privacy awareness affects platform choice; messaging apps (WhatsApp, Telegram) are significant for information spread; Facebook remains important for older demographics
- **France**: Twitter has higher penetration among journalists and political class than general population; Facebook groups significant for community organisation
- **Poland**: Facebook dominates; Wykop (local platform) significant for certain demographics; Telegram growing for political content
- **Baltic States**: Russian-language platforms (VK, Odnoklassniki) significant for Russian-speaking minorities; this creates distinct information environments within the same country
- **Nordic Countries**: High platform literacy; fact-checking infrastructure more developed; Telegram and Signal growing for private discussion

### Language-Specific Amplification Networks

**The challenge**: Disinformation and coordinated campaigns often operate within language communities rather than national boundaries.

**Patterns to watch**:

- **German-language networks**: Content spreads across Germany, Austria, and German-speaking Switzerland; Austrian or Swiss accounts may be used to seed content that then spreads to German audiences
- **French-language networks**: France, Belgium, Switzerland, and francophone Africa; sophisticated operations may use African francophone accounts for amplification
- **Russian-language content in EU**: Reaches Russian-speaking minorities across multiple countries; may originate from Russia, but also from Russian-speaking communities within EU
- **English as bridge language**: Content translated to English can jump between language communities more easily

**Analysis implications**:
- Spread analysis must consider language communities, not administrative boundaries
- Accounts from other countries in the same language community are not automatically "foreign interference"
- Translation patterns can indicate intentional cross-border spread

### Cross-Border Spread Patterns

**How content moves across European borders**:

1. **Within language communities**: Fastest spread; no translation barrier; shared cultural references
2. **Via translation**: Slower but extends reach; translation quality can indicate effort level
3. **Via shared events**: EU-level events create cross-border attention (elections, policy debates)
4. **Via diaspora networks**: Diaspora communities connect countries through personal networks

**Signals of coordinated cross-border spread** (medium confidence indicators):
- Professionally translated content appearing simultaneously in multiple languages
- Cross-platform coordination that spans language barriers
- Timing aligned with multiple national contexts (e.g., appearing before elections in several countries)
- Networks that connect otherwise unconnected language communities

**Signals that may look suspicious but aren't**:
- Content spreading in one language community across national borders (normal)
- News about EU-level events spreading across Europe (normal)
- Diaspora communities sharing content between origin and residence countries (normal)

### The DSA and Spread Analysis

The Digital Services Act creates new transparency requirements that affect spread analysis:

**Researcher data access**:
- Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) must provide data access to vetted researchers
- This may improve ability to analyse spread patterns with platform-level data
- Access is still being implemented; availability varies

**Advertising transparency**:
- Political advertising must disclose funding sources
- This can help identify coordinated political communication campaigns
- Gaps remain for non-political but manipulative advertising

**Algorithmic transparency**:
- VLOPs must document how algorithms affect content distribution
- This can inform analysis of algorithmic vs coordinated amplification
- Full algorithm access is not provided; transparency is partial

**Practical limitations**:
- Implementation varies across platforms and member states
- Enforcement is still developing
- Cross-border coordination on enforcement remains challenging

### Implications for Practitioners

**Adjust analysis for context**:
- Know which platforms matter in your focus country/community
- Understand language community dynamics that cross borders
- Don't apply US-centric platform assumptions to European contexts

**Build appropriate networks**:
- Fact-checkers and analysts in other countries covering the same language community
- Understanding of platform usage in your focus communities
- Awareness of local/regional platforms that may not be on your radar

**Avoid overclaiming**:
- Cross-border spread within language communities is not automatically "foreign interference"
- EU integration means much legitimate cross-border information flow
- Be precise about what "foreign" means in an EU context (outside EU? Outside country? Outside language community?)

## Expert Perspective: Research on Viral Spread

### What Academic Research Tells Us

Understanding what drives virality is essential for assessing whether a particular spread pattern is suspicious or expected. Academic research provides important context, though with significant uncertainties.

### The Emotional Drivers of Sharing

**Research findings** (medium-high confidence):

Content that triggers high-arousal emotions spreads more than content triggering low-arousal emotions:

- **High-arousal positive**: Awe, excitement, amusement
- **High-arousal negative**: Anger, anxiety, outrage
- **Low-arousal** (spreads less): Sadness, contentment

**Implications for analysis**:
- Emotionally provocative content going viral is not itself evidence of manipulation
- Content designed to trigger anger or outrage has inherent viral advantage
- This doesn't mean all viral emotional content is manipulative—it means emotional content has structural advantages in attention competition

**The nuance**: Sophisticated manipulation exploits these dynamics, but so does effective legitimate communication. Emotional resonance is not a diagnostic criterion for manipulation.

### Network Structure and Spread

**Research findings** (medium confidence):

Spread patterns depend heavily on network structure:

- **Highly connected nodes** (influencers, news accounts) can create rapid broadcast spread
- **Clustered communities** can create rapid spread within the cluster, slower spread across clusters
- **Bridge accounts** (connected to multiple communities) can carry content across otherwise separate networks
- **Weak ties** (acquaintances rather than close connections) can be important for spreading content to new audiences

**Implications for analysis**:
- The same content can spread very differently depending on where it's seeded
- Early spread through highly connected accounts accelerates total reach
- Spread that remains within one cluster may indicate community-specific relevance
- Spread that jumps clusters may indicate broad appeal or intentional bridge targeting

### The Attention Economy

**The structural context**:

Virality occurs within an attention economy where:

- Content competes for limited user attention
- Platforms profit from engagement, creating incentives to promote engaging content
- Emotional, controversial, and identity-relevant content has competitive advantage
- Speed matters: first content to capture attention on a topic has advantages

**Implications for spread analysis**:
- Manipulation campaigns operate within the same attention economy as legitimate content
- Platform incentives may amplify both manipulative and authentic viral content
- The question isn't whether content exploits attention dynamics (most viral content does) but whether the content itself is deceptive
- Criticising content for "going viral" or "exploiting emotions" conflates effectiveness with manipulation

### Research Limitations and Open Questions

**What we don't know well**:

- How much viral spread is algorithm-driven vs network-driven vs content-driven (this varies and is difficult to measure)
- Whether interventions (labelling, removal, debunking) consistently reduce spread or sometimes amplify it
- How spread dynamics differ across cultures and languages
- How emerging platforms change fundamental spread dynamics

**Open research questions**:

- Can spread pattern analysis reliably distinguish coordinated from organic spread without platform-level data?

## Historical Context: From Chain Letters to Algorithms

### Pre-Digital Spread Patterns

Understanding historical information spread provides perspective on what has changed and what remains constant.

**Chain letters and rumour cascades**:

Before digital networks, information still spread through social networks:
- Chain letters exploited social obligation and superstition for spread
- Rumours spread through word of mouth, accelerated by telephone
- Propaganda spread through controlled media, but also through informal networks
- The "six degrees of separation" concept emerged from studying these patterns

**Key characteristics of pre-digital spread**:
- Slower than digital, but still capable of reaching large audiences
- Geographic constraints shaped spread patterns
- Gatekeepers (editors, broadcasters) controlled mass communication
- Personal networks enabled spread outside gatekeeper control, but at smaller scale

**What stayed constant**:
- Emotional content spread more than neutral content
- Social proof influenced belief ("everyone's saying...") 
- Trust in source mattered more than evidence quality for most people
- Identity-relevant information spread within identity communities

### The Algorithmic Shift

**What changed with social media** (1995-2015):

- Gatekeepers lost control of distribution (anyone can broadcast)
- Geographic constraints collapsed (content can reach anyone with internet access)
- Speed increased dramatically (global spread in hours, not days or weeks)
- Feedback loops created (viral content gets more distribution, creating more virality)

**What changed with algorithmic curation** (2015-present):

- Platforms began actively shaping what users see based on predicted engagement
- Content doesn't reach all followers; algorithmic selection determines visibility
- Engagement metrics became key to visibility, creating incentives for engagement-optimised content
- Personalisation created different information environments for different users

**The current environment**:

- Combination of broadcast potential (anyone can post) and algorithmic gatekeeping (platforms shape what's seen)
- Tension between platform incentives (engagement) and societal interests (accurate information)
- Rapid platform evolution (TikTok's rise, Twitter/X's changes, new platforms emerging)
- Increasing fragmentation as users spread across more platforms

### Current Challenges

**The authenticity problem**:
- Increasingly difficult to distinguish authentic from inauthentic content
- AI-generated text, images, and video approaching human-indistinguishable quality
- Authentic accounts can be compromised or purchased
- The concept of "authentic" becomes complicated when AI assists human communication

**The attribution problem**:
- Actors have learned to obscure their origins
- Domestic actors can appear foreign and vice versa
- Proxies and cutouts create plausible deniability
- Definitively attributing campaigns to specific actors is increasingly difficult

**The intervention problem**:
- Platform actions (removal, labelling) can have unintended effects
- Censorship concerns limit what platforms will do
- Whack-a-mole dynamics as content migrates across platforms
- The same interventions don't work equally across contexts

**The scale problem**:
- Volume of content exceeds human review capacity
- Automated systems have high error rates
- Speed of spread often exceeds speed of response
- Cross-platform, cross-language campaigns require coordination that's difficult to achieve

### Implications for Your Work

**Historical perspective provides**:
- Recognition that virality isn't new—the mechanisms are
- Understanding that human psychology around information is relatively constant
- Awareness that current methods will need to evolve as the environment changes
- Humility about the limits of analysis and intervention

**Practical implications**:
- Don't assume every new spread pattern indicates new manipulation
- Do update your methods as platforms and tactics evolve
- Recognise that some challenges may not have good solutions with current tools
- Build adaptable frameworks rather than rigid rules

## Quick Reference: Spread Assessment Checklist

### Rapid Assessment Protocol (15-minute version)

**Step 1: Context check (3 minutes)**

- [ ] Is there a real-world trigger for this content spreading now?
- [ ] Does the content have inherent viral properties (emotional, identity-relevant, shareable)?
- [ ] Is the spread volume unusual for this type of content?

If clear trigger + viral properties + typical volume: likely organic. Note and move on.
If no trigger + sudden spike: proceed to Step 2.

**Step 2: Origin analysis (4 minutes)**

- [ ] Can you identify the original post?
- [ ] What is the account history of the original poster?
- [ ] Does the original post show signs of inauthenticity?

Authentic origin + organic spread: likely organic.
Inauthentic origin OR unclear origin: proceed to Step 3.

**Step 3: Spread pattern (5 minutes)**

- [ ] Is spread through network (peer-to-peer) or broadcast (few accounts to many)?
- [ ] Are there timestamp clusters suggesting coordination?
- [ ] Are sharing accounts predominantly authentic or suspicious?
- [ ] Is engagement consistent with claimed audience (real comments, varied reactions)?

**Step 4: Preliminary assessment (3 minutes)**

- [ ] Form hypothesis: organic / coordinated / mixed / unclear
- [ ] Assign confidence: low / medium / high
- [ ] Identify what evidence would change your assessment
- [ ] Determine next action: close / monitor / investigate further / escalate

### Confidence Level Guide

**High confidence organic**:
- Clear trigger for spread
- Authentic, established account origin
- Spread pattern matches content type
- Engagement is substantive and varied
- No coordination signals

**Medium confidence organic**:
- Plausible trigger but not definitive
- Authentic origin
- Some unusual spread elements but explainable
- Minor suspicious accounts in spread but not driving it

**Unclear / mixed**:
- Mixed signals across categories
- Some coordination signals present
- Organic and suspicious elements both significant
- Evidence could support multiple hypotheses

**Medium confidence coordinated**:
- Timestamp clustering without natural explanation
- Multiple suspicious accounts in early spread
- Spread pattern inconsistent with content reach expectations
- Some authentic accounts involved, but suspicious accounts appear instrumental

**High confidence coordinated**:
- Clear timestamp coordination across unconnected accounts
- Predominantly inauthentic accounts in spread
- Evidence of cross-platform coordination
- Pattern matches known campaign tactics
- No plausible organic explanation

### Common Errors to Avoid

**Overclaiming coordination**:
- Viral content is not evidence of coordination (virality is organic too)
- Emotional content is not evidence of coordination (emotion drives organic sharing)
- Content you dislike is not evidence of coordination (bias check)
- Rapid spread is not evidence of coordination (some content spreads rapidly organically)

**Underclaiming coordination**:
- Don't dismiss timestamp clustering as coincidence without checking
- Don't ignore patterns of suspicious accounts if present
- Don't assume authentic content means organic spread (authentic content can be coordinated)
- Don't stop investigating because you found some organic elements

**Attribution errors**:
- Coordination signals suggest coordination, not specific actors
- "Foreign" requires evidence, not assumption
- Domestic actors can coordinate too
- Attribution confidence is separate from coordination confidence

## Stop Rule

Stop spread analysis when:
- **Clear organic explanation**: Real-world trigger + natural viral properties
- **Timebox reached**: 45 minutes without strong signals → document and monitor
- **Harm assessment is more urgent**: Sometimes you respond to content regardless of how it spread
- **Platform-level data needed**: You've exhausted what public data can tell you

## DIM Mapping

Spread pattern informs intervention:

**Organic viral spread**:
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): If content is harmful, inoculate future audiences
- **Gen 2** (Debunking): Correction can spread through same networks
- Moderation less appropriate for organic speech

**Coordinated amplification**:
- **Gen 4** (Moderation): Platform enforcement on coordinated behaviour
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Algorithmic changes to reduce artificial amplification
- Attribution work may be warranted

**Algorithmic amplification** (platform-driven):
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Pressure for algorithmic changes
- **Gen 4** (Moderation): Recommend policy for engagement-bait content

## Key Takeaway

Viral spread is not evidence of manipulation. Content goes viral because it's engaging, timely, or identity-relevant—or because it's amplified artificially. Your job is to distinguish these, not to assume the worst.
