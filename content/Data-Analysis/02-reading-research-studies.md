## Purpose

You'll learn to evaluate research claims—not to become a peer reviewer, but to know when research supports the claims made about it.

---

## Scenario

Someone cites "a study shows..." to support their claim. Before accepting this evidence, you need to assess its strength. You have:

- **Time available**: 15 minutes for basic evaluation
- **Stakes**: Research citations give claims false authority if misused
- **Data access**: Abstract, maybe full paper
- **Constraint**: You're evaluating the citation, not replicating the research

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: Does this research support the specific claim being made?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: Accept the citation, investigate further, or question it?

---

## Method: Research Evaluation Workflow

### Start here: Check the citation match

- Does the study actually say what the citation claims?
- Are limitations being ignored?
- Is the study being extrapolated beyond its scope?

**Most common problem**: Study says X under conditions Y; cited as proof of X universally.

### Step 2: Assess study quality

- Is it peer-reviewed? (Where published?)
- What's the study design? (RCT, observational, survey?)
- Sample size and representativeness?

### Step 3: Check for replication

- Is this a single study or replicated finding?
- Do other researchers find similar results?
- Is there a systematic review?

---

## Study Hierarchy

**Stronger evidence** (for causal claims):
- Systematic reviews/meta-analyses
- Randomised controlled trials
- Large prospective cohort studies

**Medium evidence**:
- Case-control studies
- Cross-sectional studies
- Well-conducted surveys

**Weaker evidence** (for causation):
- Case studies
- Expert opinion
- Preliminary/unreplicated findings

**Context matters**: Study design should match the claim being made.

---

## Common Citation Problems

**Cherry-picking**: Citing one study, ignoring contradicting research
**Overclaiming**: Study shows association, cited as proof of causation
**Outdated citation**: Newer research has superseded
**Misrepresentation**: Study doesn't say what citation claims
**Journal prestige fallacy**: Good journal ≠ every paper is right

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong research evidence**:
- Replicated across multiple studies
- Appropriate design for the claim
- Properly cited (not overclaimed)
- Consistent with broader evidence base

**Medium evidence**:
- Single well-designed study
- Awaiting replication
- Some limitations acknowledged

**Weak evidence**:
- Preliminary/unreplicated
- Poor design for claimed conclusion
- Contradicted by other research
- Misrepresented in citation

---

## Stop Rule

Stop research evaluation when:
- **Clear mismatch**: Citation doesn't support claim
- **Citation checks out**: Study supports claim appropriately
- **Need expertise**: Technical assessment beyond your capability
- **Systematic review exists**: Go to that instead of individual studies

## Key Takeaway

"A study shows..." is often misused. Check: Does the study actually say that? What are its limitations? Is it replicated? A single study rarely proves anything; a body of replicated research is stronger evidence.

---

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: An article claims "Study proves X causes Y." The study is observational, shows correlation, has modest sample size, and hasn't been replicated.

**Question**: How do you evaluate this citation?

### Your answer should include:

1. **What the study can** and cannot prove
2. **What "observational" means** for causation claims
3. **What replication status** implies
4. **How you'd express** appropriate confidence

---

**Penalty for**: Accepting all "study shows" claims, ignoring study design
**Reward for**: Citation-claim matching, replication awareness, appropriate uncertainty
