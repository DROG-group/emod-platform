## Purpose

You'll learn to evaluate poll results—understanding what they can and cannot tell you.

---

## Scenario

A poll result is cited in political debate. Before treating it as fact, you need to assess its reliability and meaning. You have:

- **Time available**: 10 minutes for evaluation
- **Stakes**: Polls shape perception of public opinion
- **Data access**: Poll results, some methodology if available
- **Constraint**: Polls measure opinion at a moment, not truth

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: How reliable is this poll for this purpose?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: Trust, question, or seek more polls?

---

## Method: Poll Evaluation Workflow

### Start here: Check the source

- Who conducted the poll? (Partisan or independent?)
- Who paid for it?
- What's their track record?

### Step 2: Check methodology

- How were respondents selected? (Random vs convenience)
- Sample size and margin of error?
- Response rate and weighting?

### Step 3: Check question wording

- Is the question neutral or leading?
- Are options balanced?
- What context was provided?

---

## Key Polling Concepts

**Margin of error**:
- Tells you the uncertainty range
- "45% ± 3%" means 42-48% is plausible
- Only covers sampling error, not other problems

**Sample vs population**:
- Poll samples a subset
- Representativeness matters more than size
- 1,000 well-selected > 10,000 self-selected

**Likely vs registered vs all adults**:
- Different populations, different results
- "Likely voters" involves modeling assumptions
- Compare like with like

**Question effects**:
- Wording dramatically affects results
- Order of questions matters
- Options provided shape answers

---

## Polling strong signals

**Strong signals** (unreliable poll):
- Sponsor has stake in result
- Non-random sample (online volunteers, etc.)
- Leading question wording
- No methodology disclosed
- Results far outside other polls

**Medium signals** (use with caution):
- Partisan pollster (not always wrong, but potential bias)
- Small sample size for subgroups
- Unusual weighting choices

**Weak signals** (not automatically problematic):
- Results you disagree with
- Single poll differs from aggregates (could be noise)

---

## Stop Rule

Stop poll evaluation when:
- **Clear problems**: Methodological issues identified
- **Poll checks out**: Reputable source, good methodology
- **Poll average available**: Individual poll less important
- **Not actually news**: Small changes within margin of error

## Key Takeaway

Polls measure opinion at a moment with uncertainty. Check: Who did it? How? What exactly was asked? Margin of error matters. One poll is less meaningful than poll aggregates. Results within margin of error of each other are consistent, not contradictory.

---

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: Two polls on the same topic: Poll A shows 48% support, Poll B shows 52% support. Headlines declare "polls contradict each other." Both have ±3% margin of error.

**Question**: Do these polls actually contradict each other?

### Your answer should include:

1. **What margin of error means** for this comparison
2. **Whether contradiction is real** or within expected variation
3. **What you'd want to know** about each poll
4. **Better interpretation** of these results

---

**Penalty for**: Treating small differences as meaningful, ignoring methodology
**Reward for**: Margin of error understanding, appropriate uncertainty
