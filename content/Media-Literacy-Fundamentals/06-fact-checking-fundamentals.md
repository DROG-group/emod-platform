## Purpose

You'll learn when and how to fact-check claims—not to verify everything, but to make efficient decisions about what deserves verification.

**Output format**: Assessment → Confidence (low/med/high) → Next action

---

## Scenario

A claim crosses your feed that seems important but questionable. Before sharing, researching, or ignoring, you need to decide: is this worth fact-checking? You have:

- **Time available**: 5 minutes for triage, more if warranted
- **Stakes**: Varies by claim importance and your reach
- **Data access**: Search engines, fact-check databases
- **Constraint**: You can't fact-check everything—prioritisation matters

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: Is this claim verifiable, and is it worth your time?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High (after checking)
3. **Next action**: Share, don't share, or share with context?

---

## Method: Fact-Check Workflow

### Start here: Triage—should you fact-check this?

Prioritise fact-checking when:
- You're about to share or act on the claim
- The claim is consequential if true
- The claim is falsifiable (can be checked)
- You have reason to doubt it

**Don't fact-check**: Opinion, unfalsifiable claims, low-stakes content, things you're not sharing anyway.

### Step 2: Check if someone already did the work

Before original research:
- Search fact-checking databases (Snopes, PolitiFact, Full Fact, etc.)
- Search the claim in quotes
- Check if original source has been verified or debunked

**Most viral claims have already been checked.**

### Step 3: Go to primary sources

If no existing fact-check:
- Find the original source of the claim
- Verify against authoritative sources
- Check if claim is being accurately represented

---

## Fact-Check Principles

**Verify, don't just search for confirmation**:
- Look for disconfirming evidence
- Check multiple independent sources
- Be willing to find your suspicions wrong

**Primary sources over secondary**:
- Original study over news report
- Official statement over interpretation
- Raw data over analysis (when accessible)

**Consider claim type**:
- Factual claims: Verifiable against evidence
- Predictions: Can't be fact-checked yet
- Opinions: Not claims of fact
- Values: Not empirically checkable

## Case Study 1: The Viral Health Claim Fact-Check

### Background

Health misinformation is among the most dangerous and common categories requiring fact-checking. This case examines the process of checking a viral health claim from initial encounter to verdict.

### The Scenario

A viral post claims: "Study proves that [common medication] causes [serious side effect] in 40% of users—your doctor isn't telling you!"

The post has 50,000 shares. It cites "new research" without specific attribution.

### Fact-Check Process

**Step 1: Triage decision** (2 minutes)

Worth checking because:
- Health claims are high-stakes
- Specific percentage makes it falsifiable
- Viral spread means potential harm
- Suspicious framing ("your doctor isn't telling you")

**Step 2: Search existing fact-checks** (3 minutes)

Search: "[medication name]" + "40%" + "fact check"

Results:
- Snopes article addresses this specific claim
- AFP Fact Check covered it
- Multiple fact-checkers rated it "Misleading" or "False"

**Step 3: What the fact-checkers found**

- The cited "study" was a preliminary report, not peer-reviewed
- "40%" was for a minor, temporary side effect (headache), not the serious side effect claimed
- The viral post conflated different side effects
- Regulatory agencies (FDA, EMA) reviewed the medication and found acceptable safety profile

**Verdict**: False/Misleading
- A study exists (partially true)
- 40% figure exists but for different outcome (misleading context)
- "Serious side effect" claim is not supported (false)

### Key Lessons

1. **Specific numbers can be real but misapplied**: The 40% was real; the association with serious harm was not
2. **Existing fact-checks save time**: Multiple organizations had already verified
3. **Primary source check reveals manipulation**: The actual study didn't support the viral claim
4. **Framing is part of the misinformation**: "Your doctor isn't telling you" is emotional manipulation

## Case Study 2: The Political Quote Verification

### Background

Quotes attributed to politicians are frequently fabricated, taken out of context, or edited to change meaning. This case examines verifying a viral political quote.

### The Scenario

A quote circulates attributed to a prominent politician: "[Controversial statement that aligns with critics' characterization]"

The quote appears as text on an image. No source, date, or context is provided.

### Fact-Check Process

**Step 1: Search the exact quote** (2 minutes)

Put the quote in quotation marks and search.

**Possible outcomes**:

1. **No results**: Quote may be fabricated (or too new to index)
2. **Results from partisan sites only**: Suspicious; may be fabricated or distorted
3. **Results from news organizations**: Check what they actually reported
4. **Video/transcript match**: Strongest verification

**Step 2: Find original context** (5 minutes)

If quote appears real:
- Find the full transcript or video
- Check what was said before and after
- Assess whether quote is representative of full statement

**Step 3: Common manipulation patterns**

| Pattern | Example | How to Spot |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Fabrication | Quote never said | No primary source exists |
| Out of context | Sarcasm presented as serious | Full video shows different meaning |
| Splicing | Two statements combined | Jump cuts, ellipses without [...]  |
| Paraphrase as quote | "They basically said" becomes direct quote | Original doesn't match |
| Old quote, new context | 2010 quote presented as response to 2024 event | Check date |

### Example Outcome

**Search reveals**: Quote appears only on partisan meme sites and social media. No video, transcript, or credible news source contains it.

**Assessment**: High confidence this is fabricated. No evidence the politician said this.

**If quote had been real but out of context**: Would note that quote exists but misrepresents full statement. Context changes meaning.

### Key Lessons

1. **Text-on-image quotes are high-risk**: Easy to fabricate, share without attribution
2. **Search the exact quote first**: Fastest way to check existence
3. **Context can be as important as accuracy**: Real quote can be misleading
4. **Absence of primary source is significant**: Real quotes can be traced

## Case Study 3: The "Everyone Is Saying" Claim

### Background

Some viral claims don't have a clear origin—they emerge as "everyone knows" or "people are saying" without specific attribution. These are harder to fact-check but follow patterns.

### The Scenario

A claim circulates: "Scientists are now saying that [established scientific consensus] is wrong."

No specific scientists, studies, or institutions are named.

### Fact-Check Approach

**Step 1: Identify what would make this true**

For this claim to be accurate, you'd expect:
- Named scientists making this claim
- Published papers challenging consensus
- Coverage in scientific journals/conferences
- Institutional responses

**Step 2: Search for expected evidence**

Search: "[topic]" + "scientists challenge" OR "new research contradicts"

**Possible findings**:

1. **No credible sources**: The "scientists are saying" is fabricated
2. **A few outlier scientists**: Not "scientists" broadly; may be credentialed contrarians
3. **Preliminary/contested research**: Real but overstated; debate exists but consensus hasn't shifted
4. **Actual paradigm shift**: Rare, but sometimes consensus does change

**Step 3: Check scientific consensus sources**

For scientific claims:
- IPCC for climate (consensus position)
- WHO for health (consensus position)
- National academies of science
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Compare viral claim against established consensus documents.

### Example Outcome

**Search reveals**: The claim traces to a single scientist with known contrarian positions, amplified by advocacy organizations. No scientific societies have changed their positions. The "new research" is a preprint that hasn't been peer-reviewed.

**Assessment**: Misleading. A scientist (singular) made a claim, but "scientists are now saying" implies broader shift that hasn't occurred.

### Key Lessons

1. **"Scientists say" requires named scientists**: Vague attribution is often false
2. **Outliers exist in every field**: One scientist ≠ scientific consensus
3. **Check institutional consensus**: Academies, major journals, systematic reviews
4. **Preprints aren't peer-reviewed**: Real but not validated

## Tool Tutorial: Fact-Check Database Search

### Major Fact-Checking Organizations

**International/English**:
- Snopes (snopes.com) - Long-running, wide coverage
- AFP Fact Check (factcheck.afp.com) - Global wire service
- Reuters Fact Check (reuters.com/fact-check) - News agency
- Full Fact (fullfact.org) - UK-focused
- PolitiFact (politifact.com) - US politics focus
- FactCheck.org - US focus, Annenberg project

**EU-Specific**:
- EUvsDisinfo (euvsdisinfo.eu) - Pro-Kremlin disinformation (note: advocacy source)
- AFP Fact Check in multiple EU languages
- National fact-checkers (Les Décodeurs/France, DPA/Germany, Maldita/Spain, etc.)

**Aggregators**:
- Google Fact Check Explorer (toolbox.google.com/factcheck/explorer)
- Duke Reporters' Lab database (reporterslab.org/fact-checking/)
- IFCN Signatories list (ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org)

### Step-by-Step Search

**Step 1: Google Fact Check Explorer**

1. Go to toolbox.google.com/factcheck/explorer
2. Enter key terms from the claim
3. Review results from multiple fact-checkers
4. Check date (old fact-checks may need updating)

**Step 2: Direct organization search**

If Google Fact Check doesn't find it:
1. Go to major fact-checker site (Snopes, AFP, etc.)
2. Use their internal search
3. Try different phrasings of the claim

**Step 3: General search**

Search: "[claim]" + "fact check" OR "debunked" OR "false"

**Caution**: Not everything labeled "fact check" is from credible organizations. Check the source.

### Evaluating Fact-Checkers

**IFCN Certification**:
- International Fact-Checking Network certifies signatories
- Signatories commit to transparency, corrections, nonpartisanship
- Not a guarantee of perfection, but a quality signal

**What to look for**:
- Methodology transparency (how do they verify?)
- Source citation (can you check their work?)
- Corrections policy (do they fix errors?)
- Funding transparency (who pays for this?)

**What to watch for**:
- Partisan fact-checkers (exist on all sides)
- "Fact-check" labels on opinion pieces
- Cherry-picking what to check
- Lack of sourcing

## Tool Tutorial: Primary Source Verification

### Finding Primary Sources

**For quotes/statements**:
- Official transcripts (government sites, C-SPAN, Hansard)
- Video archives (YouTube, official channels)
- Press release archives
- News organization footage

**For studies/research**:
- PubMed for medical research
- Google Scholar for academic papers
- Journal websites
- Preprint servers (with caution—not peer-reviewed)

**For statistics**:
- Eurostat, national statistics offices
- World Bank, OECD data portals
- Original survey/study data
- Government reports

### Verification Steps

**Step 1: Trace the claim to origin**

Follow the citation chain:
- Article cites a report
- Report cites a study
- Study has primary data

Go to the end of the chain.

**Step 2: Check primary source says what's claimed**

Common disconnects:
- Study conclusion doesn't match headline
- Quote is truncated or out of context
- Data doesn't support interpretation

**Step 3: Assess primary source quality**

- Is it peer-reviewed?
- Is it from a credible institution?
- Is it recent and relevant?
- Has it been replicated?

## Practice Exercise 1: The Breaking News Claim

### Scenario

A breaking news story claims: "City Mayor arrested for fraud—$10 million missing"

The story appears on a site you don't recognize. It's being shared widely. Other news sources haven't covered it yet.

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. Should you fact-check this? Why?
2. Walk through your verification process
3. What would make you confident it's true vs false?
4. What would you do if you can't verify?

### Worked Solution

**Triage decision**:

Worth checking because:
- Consequential if true (major political news)
- Falsifiable (arrest is a public record)
- Suspicious that major outlets haven't covered
- Source is unfamiliar

**Verification process**:

**Step 1: Check original source** (2 minutes)
- Who runs this site?
- Is there an author byline?
- Is there contact information?
- What else does this site publish?

**Step 2: Search for corroboration** (3 minutes)
- Search mayor's name + "arrested"
- Check local news outlets
- Check official city/police sources
- Check wire services (AP, Reuters)

**Step 3: Check public records** (5 minutes)
- Police arrest logs (if publicly accessible)
- Court records
- Official statements from city

**Possible outcomes**:

| Finding | Interpretation | Action |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| No other sources, fake site | Likely fabrication | Don't share |
| Local news confirms | True | Can share with attribution |
| Legitimate site, but unconfirmed | Too early to tell | Wait for confirmation |
| Partial truth (different charges) | Misleading | Don't share as-is |

**If you can't verify**:

- Don't share
- Don't add "if true" (still amplifies unverified claim)
- Wait for credible sources
- Note that you checked and couldn't confirm

## Practice Exercise 2: The Scientific Study Claim

### Scenario

An article claims: "New study proves [controversial conclusion about diet/health]"

The article links to a study. The conclusion seems to contradict established advice.

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. How do you evaluate whether the study supports the claim?
2. What questions do you ask about the study itself?
3. How do you assess if this changes the scientific consensus?
4. What would you conclude if the study is real but the claim overstated?

### Worked Solution

**Step 1: Access the actual study** (3 minutes)

- Click through to the study link
- Read the abstract and conclusion
- Note: Does study conclusion match article headline?

**Step 2: Evaluate study quality** (5 minutes)

Questions to ask:
- Is it peer-reviewed or a preprint?
- How large is the sample size?
- Is it observational or controlled experiment?
- Who funded it?
- What do the authors actually conclude (vs what's claimed)?

**strong signals**:
- Preprint (not yet peer-reviewed)
- Very small sample
- Funded by interested party
- Authors' conclusion more cautious than article claims

**Step 3: Check consensus** (5 minutes)

- What do major health organizations say?
- What do systematic reviews conclude?
- Is this one study or part of larger body?
- Have experts in the field commented?

**Possible verdicts**:

| Scenario | Verdict |
|----------|---------|
| Study is real, but misrepresented | "Misleading"—study doesn't say what's claimed |
| Study is real and accurately described but outlier | "True but needs context"—one study ≠ consensus |
| Study is real and represents genuine new evidence | "True, more research needed"—but rare |
| Study doesn't exist or says opposite | "False" |

**Appropriate response to real but overstated study**:

"A new study found [actual finding], but this doesn't prove [stronger claim]. Scientific consensus based on [broader evidence] still supports [established advice]. More research is needed to confirm these preliminary findings."

## Practice Exercise 3: The Historical Claim

### Scenario

A viral post claims: "[Historical figure] actually said/did [surprising thing that changes how we understand them]"

The claim is presented as recently discovered truth that "they don't teach in schools."

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. How do you approach historical claims differently?
2. What sources would you check?
3. What makes you more or less confident?
4. How do you handle genuine historical complexity?

### Worked Solution

**Historical claim challenges**:

- Primary sources may be harder to access
- Historical context matters
- Interpretation varies among historians
- "They don't teach you" framing is often propaganda

**Verification approach**:

**Step 1: Check if historians have addressed this** (5 minutes)

- Academic sources (JSTOR, Google Scholar)
- Reputable historical organizations
- University history departments
- Historian commentary on viral claims

**Step 2: Seek primary sources** (5 minutes)

- Original documents (if accessible)
- Contemporary accounts
- Academic biographies
- Museum/archive resources

**Step 3: Assess claim framing** (3 minutes)

- Is this genuinely new information or old claim recycled?
- Does "they don't teach you" signal legitimate omission or manufactured controversy?
- Who benefits from this narrative?

**Confidence assessment**:

| Evidence | Confidence Level |
|----------|------------------|
| Primary source confirms, historians agree | High |
| Historians debate, evidence mixed | Medium—present complexity |
| No evidence, historians reject | Low—likely false |
| Claim too vague to verify | Unable to rate |

**Handling genuine complexity**:

History is often more complex than simple claims suggest. If evidence shows:
- Partial truth: Acknowledge what's accurate and what's missing
- Context matters: Explain what changes interpretation
- Scholarly debate exists: Note the disagreement honestly

Don't flatten genuine complexity into false/true binary when the truth is "it's complicated."

## Expert Perspective: Fact-Checking Effectiveness and Limits

### Research on Fact-Check Effectiveness

**Does fact-checking work?**

**The good news** (various studies):
- Fact-checks do reduce belief in false claims
- Effect persists over time (though diminishes)
- Works across political orientations
- Trusted fact-checkers are more effective

**The challenges**:
- Corrections spread less than original claims
- Some people resist corrections (backfire effect, though less common than feared)
- Fact-checks arrive after damage done
- Not everyone encounters the correction

### Practitioner Insights

**From professional fact-checkers**:
- "Speed matters, but accuracy matters more"
- "We can't check everything—we choose what has most potential harm"
- "The hardest claims are partially true but misleading"
- "Explaining methodology builds trust"

**On prioritization**:
- High-harm topics (health, elections) get priority
- Viral reach matters (fact-checking before something goes viral is more effective)
- Recurring claims are worth addressing even if old
- Some claims aren't worth amplifying by fact-checking

### Limits of Fact-Checking

**What fact-checking can't do**:
- Change deeply held beliefs immediately
- Reach everyone who saw the original claim
- Check faster than misinformation spreads
- Address opinion or values (only factual claims)

**What complements fact-checking**:
- Prebunking (Gen 3)—warn before exposure
- Media literacy education—teach the skill
- Platform design—reduce spread of false claims
- Source credibility signals—help users evaluate themselves

## Historical Context: The Rise of Fact-Checking

### Pre-Institutional Era

**Before organized fact-checking**:
- Newsrooms had internal fact-checkers (still do)
- Corrections were occasional and not systematic
- Claims persisted without structured challenge
- Expertise-based authority was less questioned

### Formalization (2000s-2010s)

**Key developments**:
- FactCheck.org founded 2003
- PolitiFact founded 2007 (Pulitzer Prize 2009)
- Snopes transitioned from urban legends to political claims
- Academic study of fact-checking began

**What drove growth**:
- Political polarization increased false claims
- Social media accelerated misinformation spread
- Platforms began partnering with fact-checkers
- Audience demand for verification

### Current State

**Platform integration**:
- Facebook, Google, Twitter/X have (had) fact-check partnerships
- Disputed labels on some content
- Search result prioritization

**Challenges**:
- Scale—can't check everything
- Speed—misinformation outpaces checking
- Trust—fact-checkers are themselves distrusted by some
- Business models—sustainability questions

### Future Trajectory

**Emerging approaches**:
- AI-assisted claim detection and checking
- Community notes / crowdsourced fact-checking
- Proactive monitoring (catch claims before viral)
- Integration with media literacy education

**Persistent challenges**:
- Deepfakes and AI-generated misinformation
- Cross-platform spread
- Verification in non-English languages
- Maintaining independence and trust

## Quick Reference: Fact-Check Workflow

### Triage (2 minutes)

- [ ] Is this claim falsifiable (can be checked)?
- [ ] Is it consequential if true/false?
- [ ] Am I about to share or act on it?
- [ ] Do I have reason to doubt it?

If yes to most → worth checking

### Quick Check (5 minutes)

- [ ] Search fact-check databases (Google Fact Check Explorer, Snopes, AFP)
- [ ] Search the claim in quotes
- [ ] Check if credible news sources have covered it

If already checked → use that verdict (with verification)

### Deep Check (15+ minutes)

- [ ] Find the primary source
- [ ] Verify source says what's claimed
- [ ] Check additional sources for corroboration
- [ ] Assess source quality and context
- [ ] Form evidence-based verdict

### Verdict Categories

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| True | Claim is accurate as stated |
| Mostly True | Accurate with minor error |
| Half True | Partially accurate, missing important context |
| Mostly False | More wrong than right |
| False | Claim is inaccurate |
| Unverifiable | Cannot be confirmed or denied |

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (claim likely false):
- Contradicted by multiple authoritative sources
- Original source doesn't support the claim
- Known to be false from fact-checking organisations
- Fails basic plausibility checks
- No evidence claim exists (for quotes, events)

**Medium signals** (uncertainty remains):
- Mixed evidence or competing claims
- True but misleading (missing context)
- Partially true (some elements accurate, others not)
- Real but overstated

**Weak signals** (insufficient for verdict):
- You can't find it verified (absence isn't disproof)
- Source seems unreliable (attack the argument, not source)
- Claim is surprising (surprising things happen)
- You want it to be false (bias alert)

---

## Stop Rule

Stop fact-checking when:
- **Verdict reached**: Clear evidence for or against
- **Proportionate effort**: Time spent matches stakes
- **Uncertainty acceptable**: Sometimes "unclear" is the honest answer
- **Not your expertise**: Complex claims may need experts

## DIM Mapping

Fact-checking is primarily Gen 2 intervention:

**Gen 2** (Debunking): Direct correction after false claims spread; core fact-checking function
**Gen 3** (Prebunking): Fact-checkers can contribute to inoculation materials
**Gen 4** (Moderation): Fact-check verdicts inform platform content decisions
**Gen 5** (Interaction): Fact-check integration in search/social design

**Limitations of Gen 2**:
- Slow relative to viral spread
- Correction reaches fewer people than original claim
- Doesn't prevent initial belief formation
- Resource-intensive, doesn't scale to all claims

**Combine with other generations** for comprehensive approach.

---

## Key Takeaway

Fact-checking is valuable but limited. Prioritise: claims you'll share, consequential claims, verifiable claims. Check if someone already did the work—most viral claims have been checked. Be willing to find your suspicions wrong; confirmation bias affects fact-checking too. Know when uncertainty is the honest answer. Fact-checking is one tool in a broader toolkit; combine with prebunking and media literacy for better results.
