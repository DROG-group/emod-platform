## Purpose

You'll learn to identify bias in sources—without assuming bias equals unreliability or that you're free of bias yourself.

**Output format**: Assessment → Confidence (low/med/high) → Next action

---

## Scenario

Someone dismisses a source as "biased." Before accepting or rejecting this assessment, you need to evaluate what kind of bias is present and whether it matters. You have:

- **Time available**: 15 minutes to assess bias relevance
- **Stakes**: Dismissing useful sources vs accepting distorted ones
- **Data access**: The content, information about the source
- **Constraint**: All sources have some perspective—bias isn't binary

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What type of bias is present and does it affect reliability for this claim?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: Use the source, seek alternatives, or use with caveats?

---

## Method: Bias Assessment Workflow

### Start here: Distinguish bias types

Not all bias is equal:
- **Selection bias**: What's covered vs ignored
- **Framing bias**: How stories are presented
- **Confirmation bias**: Seeking evidence for predetermined conclusions
- **Funding/conflict bias**: Financial interests affecting coverage

Different biases affect different claims differently.

### Step 2: Assess bias relevance

For the specific claim you're evaluating:
- Does the source's known bias point toward or away from this claim?
- Would the bias motivate getting this wrong?
- Are factual claims separable from framing?

**Key insight**: A biased source can be reliable on facts that don't serve its bias.

### Step 3: Calibrate your own bias

Before dismissing a source as biased:
- Would you apply the same scrutiny to sources you agree with?
- Are you using "bias" to dismiss inconvenient information?
- What's your own perspective on this topic?

---

## Types of Bias

**Ideological bias**:
- Consistent political/social perspective
- Affects framing more than basic facts
- Predictable once identified—can compensate

**Financial/conflict bias**:
- Interests that could affect coverage
- More concerning for claims directly tied to interests
- Disclosure requirements exist for a reason

**Selection bias**:
- What stories get covered
- What sources get quoted
- What context gets included

**Framing bias**:
- Which aspects are emphasised
- What language is used
- How narratives are constructed

**Omission bias**:
- What's left out
- Harder to detect than commission
- Requires consulting multiple sources

## Case Study 1: The Same Event, Different Frames (2021 Belarus Border Crisis)

### Background

In late 2021, thousands of migrants attempted to cross from Belarus into Poland, Lithuania, and Latvia. Coverage of the same events varied dramatically depending on source perspective, providing a clear example of framing bias with verifiable underlying facts.

### The Coverage Patterns

**EU/Western media framing**:
- "Hybrid warfare" by Belarus/Russia
- Migrants as "weaponised" by Lukashenko
- Focus on EU border security challenges
- Language: "orchestrated crisis," "migration weaponisation"

**Russian state media framing**:
- Humanitarian crisis caused by EU
- EU hypocrisy on human rights
- Focus on migrant suffering at borders
- Language: "humanitarian catastrophe," "EU cruelty"

**Human rights organisation framing**:
- Focus on migrant welfare regardless of geopolitics
- Criticism of both Belarus (for using people) and EU (for pushbacks)
- Language: "suffering," "rights violations," "vulnerable people"

### Analysis: What Was Factual, What Was Framing?

**Verifiable facts (high confidence)**:
- Migrants were present at the border (confirmed by all sources)
- Belarus facilitated visa and travel logistics (documented)
- Physical conditions were harsh (documented)
- Some pushback incidents occurred (documented by multiple sources)
- Migrants came primarily from Middle East (documented)

**Contested interpretations**:
- Was this "hybrid warfare"? (Framing—implies strategic intent)
- Was EU response proportionate? (Normative judgment)
- Who bears primary responsibility? (Attribution)
- What motivated Lukashenko? (Intent assessment)

**Key lesson**: The same set of facts supported multiple frames. None of the major outlets fabricated basic facts, but framing determined which facts were emphasised and how they were interpreted.

### What This Means for Bias Assessment

**When evaluating coverage of this event**:

1. **Separate facts from frame**: "Migrants are at the border" is verifiable; "EU is cruel" is interpretation
2. **Identify predictable bias**: State media serves state interests; advocacy groups have missions
3. **Use multiple frames**: Reading both EU and Russian coverage reveals what each omits
4. **Don't assume bias = wrong**: Russian media's focus on migrant suffering wasn't fabricated, even if deployed for geopolitical purposes

**Assessment template**:

| Source Type | Predictable Bias | Facts Reliable? | Framing Reliable? |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| EU media | Pro-EU security frame | Generally yes | Serves EU perspective |
| Russian state media | Anti-EU, pro-Russia frame | Often yes on specific incidents | Serves Russian narrative |
| Human rights orgs | Humanitarian frame | Generally yes | Mission-focused |

## Case Study 2: Industry-Funded Research (Sugar Industry, 1960s-2010s)

### Background

In 2016, researchers published historical analysis showing that the sugar industry funded research in the 1960s that downplayed sugar's role in heart disease and shifted blame to dietary fat. This case illustrates how funding bias can operate over decades.

### What the Evidence Showed

**Documented facts**:
- Sugar industry trade group (Sugar Research Foundation) funded Harvard researchers in 1967
- Funded research was published in New England Journal of Medicine
- Research concluded dietary fat, not sugar, was the primary concern for heart disease
- Funding was not disclosed in the original publication
- Industry documents showed SRF set the objective and reviewed drafts

### Analysis: Understanding Funding Bias

**How funding bias operated here**:

1. **Topic selection**: Research questions were shaped by funder interests (study fat, not sugar)
2. **Outcome orientation**: Researchers knew what conclusion the funder wanted
3. **Publication bias**: Results favourable to funder were published; unfavourable results might not have been
4. **Disclosure failure**: Readers couldn't assess potential bias without knowing funding source

**What this didn't mean**:
- The researchers didn't fabricate data (they selected and interpreted)
- Dietary fat isn't harmless (it has independent health effects)
- All industry-funded research is unreliable (but requires scrutiny)

**Key insight**: Funding bias often operates through selection and emphasis rather than outright fabrication. The biased research wasn't "fake"—it was incomplete and framed to serve funder interests.

### Modern Implications

**For source evaluation**:

1. **Check funding disclosure**: Who paid for this research or reporting?
2. **Assess alignment**: Does the conclusion serve the funder's interests?
3. **Look for replication**: Do independent researchers find the same results?
4. **Consider selection bias**: What research might NOT have been funded or published?

**Current examples of funding bias concern**:
- Pharmaceutical company-funded drug trials
- Tech company-funded AI safety research
- Energy company-funded climate research
- Industry-funded nutrition research

**Not automatic disqualification**: Industry-funded research can be valid, but requires higher scrutiny and ideally independent replication.

## Case Study 3: Asymmetric Bias Scrutiny in Social Media Debates

### Background

A common pattern in online discourse: people scrutinise sources supporting opposing views while accepting sources supporting their views uncritically. This case examines the pattern itself rather than a specific event.

### The Pattern

**Observed behaviour**:
- User A shares article from Source X supporting Position 1
- User B dismisses Source X as "biased" and shares Source Y supporting Position 2
- User A dismisses Source Y as "biased"
- Neither examines their own source with the scrutiny applied to the other's

**Both may be correct that the opposing source is biased—but neither is applying symmetric standards.**

### Analysis: The "Bias" Accusation as Thought-Terminator

**How "biased" functions in arguments**:

1. **Dismissal without engagement**: "That's biased" ends conversation without addressing claims
2. **Asymmetric application**: Own sources aren't labeled "biased" even when they are
3. **Binary thinking**: Assumes bias = completely unreliable
4. **Moral framing**: "Biased" implies bad faith, not just perspective

**What's missing**:
- Specificity: What type of bias? How does it affect this claim?
- Symmetry: Am I applying the same standard to sources I agree with?
- Proportionality: Does the bias actually affect the factual claims?
- Self-awareness: What's my own bias on this topic?

### Building Better Practice

**When someone calls a source "biased"**:

Ask:
- What specific bias are you identifying?
- How does that bias affect this specific claim?
- What source without this bias makes a different factual claim?
- Are you applying the same scrutiny to sources you prefer?

**When you're tempted to dismiss a source as "biased"**:

Ask yourself:
- Can I identify the specific bias operating?
- Would I accept this claim if it came from a source I prefer?
- Is the factual claim separable from the framing?
- Am I using "bias" to avoid engaging with uncomfortable information?

## Tool Tutorial: Bias Assessment Checklist

### Step-by-Step Bias Evaluation

**Step 1: Identify the source's known perspective**

For media outlets:
- Check Media Bias/Fact Check or AllSides (use as input, not final word)
- Look for "About Us" or mission statements
- Check ownership and funding
- Review historical coverage patterns

For research:
- Check funding disclosure
- Check author affiliations
- Look for prior publications by same authors

For individuals:
- Check professional background
- Look for stated positions
- Check who they work for/with

**Step 2: Determine bias type**

Ask: Which bias type applies?

| Type | Indicator | Concern Level |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Ideological | Consistent political frame | Medium—predictable, can compensate |
| Financial | Funder interests align with conclusion | High—direct incentive to distort |
| Selection | Topics covered vs ignored | Medium—affects completeness |
| Framing | Language and emphasis | Low-Medium—facts may still be accurate |

**Step 3: Assess bias relevance to specific claim**

Key question: Does this bias motivate getting THIS claim wrong?

- Claim supports source's known bias → Higher scrutiny needed
- Claim contradicts source's known bias → Higher confidence (against-interest statement)
- Claim is tangential to bias → Bias less relevant

**Step 4: Check for against-interest statements**

Powerful evidence: When a source reports something that contradicts their known bias.

Examples:
- Conservative outlet reporting Republican misconduct
- Environmental group acknowledging limits of renewable energy
- Industry acknowledging product risks

Against-interest statements warrant higher confidence because bias would push the other direction.

**Step 5: Apply symmetric standards**

Self-test questions:
- Am I checking sources I agree with as carefully as sources I disagree with?
- Would I accept this evidence quality from a source with opposite bias?
- Am I using "bias" to avoid engaging with the content?

### Tool: AllSides Media Bias Ratings

AllSides rates media outlets on a left-to-right spectrum. Usage guidance:

**What it's good for**:
- Quick orientation to a source's general perspective
- Identifying blind spots in your media diet
- Finding alternative perspectives on same story

**Limitations**:
- US-focused (limited European coverage)
- Left-right spectrum oversimplifies many biases
- Rating doesn't indicate reliability
- Bias ≠ inaccuracy

**How to use it**:
1. Check rating for general orientation
2. Don't stop there—assess relevance to specific claim
3. Find sources with different ratings covering same story
4. Compare what each includes and omits

### Tool: Ad Fontes Media Bias Chart

The "Media Bias Chart" maps outlets on two dimensions: left-right bias and reliability. Usage guidance:

**What it's good for**:
- Distinguishing bias from reliability (separate axes)
- Visual overview of media landscape
- Identifying relatively centrist and reliable sources

**Limitations**:
- US-focused
- Methodology is debated
- Placement is approximate, not precise
- Chart doesn't replace specific claim evaluation

## Practice Exercise 1: The Advocacy Organisation Report

### Scenario

You're researching housing policy. A tenants' rights advocacy organisation releases a report claiming that a new rent regulation reduced evictions by 40%. The report includes methodology and data.

A landlord association dismisses the report as "biased" and releases their own report claiming the regulation reduced housing investment by 25%.

**Your task (20 minutes)**:

1. Identify what type of bias each source has
2. Assess whether the bias affects each source's factual claims
3. Determine what you can and cannot use from each report
4. Design a symmetric evaluation process

### Constraints

- You need to reach a conclusion for a policy brief
- You cannot conduct original research
- Both reports cite data sources you can verify
- Time pressure means you can't verify everything

### Worked Solution

**Bias identification**:

| Source | Bias Type | Direction |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Tenants' rights org | Mission-driven | Favours tenant-protective policies |
| Landlord association | Financial/membership | Favours landlord-friendly policies |

Both have predictable biases. This is expected for advocacy organisations.

**Bias relevance assessment**:

*Tenants' rights claim (40% eviction reduction)*:
- Claim directly supports organisation's mission
- Bias provides motivation to present favourable interpretation
- Higher scrutiny warranted
- Check: Is the underlying data verifiable? Is the methodology transparent? Do independent sources corroborate?

*Landlord association claim (25% investment reduction)*:
- Claim directly supports organisation's interests
- Bias provides motivation to present unfavourable interpretation
- Higher scrutiny warranted
- Same questions apply

**What's usable**:

If underlying data is verifiable:
- Raw data may be reliable even if interpretation is biased
- Methodology, if transparent, can be evaluated
- Specific, measurable claims can be checked

If methodology is sound:
- Results may be accurate even from biased source
- Framing and implications may still be biased

**Symmetric evaluation process**:

Apply the same steps to both reports:
1. Can you access the underlying data?
2. Is the methodology clearly described?
3. Has the methodology been peer-reviewed or replicated?
4. Are there alternative explanations the report doesn't consider?
5. Do independent sources (academic researchers, government statistics) corroborate?

**Decision framework**:

| Scenario | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Both methodologies sound, data verifiable | Use both with appropriate caveats about source perspectives |
| One methodology questionable | Weight the other more heavily, note the issue |
| Neither independently verifiable | Seek additional sources before drawing conclusions |
| Independent sources contradict one | Weight independent source more heavily |

### Common Mistakes

1. **Dismissing advocacy source while accepting industry source (or vice versa)**: Both have bias; both need scrutiny
2. **Treating bias as disqualifying**: Biased sources can have good data
3. **Ignoring methodology in favour of source labels**: Evaluate what they did, not just who they are
4. **Failing to seek independent verification**: Neither advocacy source is ideal; look for academic or government data

## Practice Exercise 2: The News Comparison

### Scenario

The same political event is covered by three outlets:

**Outlet A** (left-leaning): "Progressive Victory: New Policy Will Help Millions of Working Families"

**Outlet B** (right-leaning): "Radical Legislation Threatens Economic Growth, Critics Warn"

**Outlet C** (centrist): "Congress Passes Major Policy Change; Impact Uncertain"

All three report the same basic facts: what the policy does, who voted for it, when it takes effect.

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. Identify what type of bias each demonstrates
2. Extract what's factual versus framed
3. Determine what a reader of only one outlet would miss
4. Assess whether any outlet is "more reliable"

### Worked Solution

**Bias identification**:

| Outlet | Bias Type | Manifestation |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Outlet A | Ideological (left) | Positive framing ("victory," "help") |
| Outlet B | Ideological (right) | Negative framing ("radical," "threatens") |
| Outlet C | Framing (caution) | Neutral framing, deferred judgment |

**Factual vs framed**:

*Factual elements (all three report)*:
- What the policy does (specific provisions)
- Vote tallies
- Effective date
- Basic description of affected populations

*Framed elements*:
- "Victory" vs "threatens" (evaluative language)
- "Working families" vs "economic growth" (emphasis choice)
- Which experts/critics are quoted
- What outcomes are predicted

**What single-source readers miss**:

*Reader of only Outlet A misses*:
- Concerns about economic impact
- Opposition arguments
- Uncertainty about outcomes

*Reader of only Outlet B misses*:
- Potential benefits
- Supporter arguments
- Uncertainty about outcomes

*Reader of only Outlet C misses*:
- Substantive arguments on either side
- Why people care
- Stakes of the decision

**Reliability assessment**:

None of the outlets is fabricating facts. All three are "reliable" for the basic facts.

For **understanding what happened**: Any would suffice
For **understanding implications**: Need multiple perspectives
For **understanding the debate**: Need A and B
For **recognising uncertainty**: C is most explicit

**Key insight**: "Centrist" isn't automatically "better." Outlet C's neutrality may underplay the genuine disagreements that A and B represent. The goal isn't to find the unbiased source (it doesn't exist) but to triangulate across biases.

## Practice Exercise 3: The Expert Disagreement

### Scenario

Two experts disagree on a scientific question:

**Expert A**: Tenured professor at major university, received industry funding for some research, concludes that Product X is safe at normal use levels.

**Expert B**: Researcher at advocacy-affiliated institute, no industry funding, concludes that Product X poses unacceptable risks and should be restricted.

Both cite peer-reviewed studies. Both have relevant credentials.

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. Identify the bias concern for each expert
2. Assess how to weight their competing claims
3. Determine what additional information you'd need
4. Avoid the trap of dismissing both as "biased"

### Worked Solution

**Bias identification**:

*Expert A*:
- Funding bias concern: Industry funding creates potential conflict
- However: Tenure provides some independence; peer review provides some check
- Bias would motivate: Finding product safe

*Expert B*:
- Mission bias concern: Advocacy affiliation suggests predetermined conclusion
- However: No direct financial conflict; may simply have different risk tolerance
- Bias would motivate: Finding product risky

**Neither expert is bias-free. The question is how bias affects their specific claims.**

**Weighting their claims**:

Questions to ask:
1. Do their studies use the same methodology? (If Expert A's methodology is better regardless of funding, that matters)
2. What does the broader scientific consensus say? (Are they outliers or representative?)
3. Are their findings replicated by independent researchers?
4. Do they have track records of accuracy or retraction?

**Against-interest check**:
- If Expert A said "my industry-funded research found unexpected risks"—high credibility
- If Expert B said "our analysis found risks are lower than we initially thought"—high credibility
- Neither is making against-interest statements, so normal scrutiny applies

**Additional information needed**:
1. Independent replication studies
2. Regulatory agency assessments
3. Meta-analyses or systematic reviews
4. Track records of both experts' previous research

**Avoiding the trap**:

Wrong conclusion: "Both are biased, so I'll ignore both"
- This leaves you with no information and doesn't resolve the question

Better approach: "Both have identifiable biases; I'll weight claims based on methodology strength, independent replication, and consistency with broader literature"

## Regional Focus: Media Bias in the EU Context

### The European Media Landscape

European media bias operates differently than the US model often used as default:

**Multi-party systems produce different bias patterns**:
- Media often aligns with specific parties in proportional systems
- "Left-right" binary oversimplifies European political spectrums
- Green, nationalist, liberal, social-democratic outlets may have distinct biases

**Public broadcasting varies significantly**:
- Some public broadcasters are highly independent (BBC, ARD, NRK)
- Others are more government-influenced (varies by country and era)
- Funding model affects independence
- Editorial independence isn't guaranteed by public ownership

**Media ownership concentration**:
- Some countries have diverse media ownership; others have concentration
- Cross-border ownership creates complex influence patterns
- Check who owns what before assuming editorial independence

### Country-Specific Considerations

**Germany**:
- Strong public broadcasting tradition (ARD, ZDF)
- Regional Länder broadcasters with local focus
- Private media includes both quality press and tabloids
- Political spectrum doesn't map cleanly to US left-right

**France**:
- Mix of public and private media
- Strong newspaper opinion tradition
- Broadcast media more regulated
- Political spectrum includes dimensions (secular-religious, pro-EU/Eurosceptic) beyond left-right

**Poland/Hungary**:
- Significant government influence on public media in recent years
- Opposition media exists but operates under pressure
- Assessment requires knowing current political context
- "Public" ≠ "independent" in current conditions

**Nordic countries**:
- Generally strong public broadcasters
- High press freedom indicators
- Smaller markets mean some concentration
- Generally high trust in media (by European standards)

### EU-Level Bias Considerations

**Pro-EU vs Eurosceptic bias**:
- Major dimension in European media
- EU institutions have communication offices with inherent bias
- Eurosceptic outlets may also have biases (often nationalist)
- Neither "pro-EU" nor "anti-EU" automatically equals reliability

**Brussels press corps**:
- Specialist reporters covering EU institutions
- May have "access" bias (maintain relationships with sources)
- Deep knowledge but also potential capture
- Important to distinguish correspondent reporting from institutional communication

### Assessing Bias in EU-Focused Coverage

**Questions to ask**:
1. Is this outlet generally pro-EU or Eurosceptic? (Affects framing)
2. Is the journalist a Brussels correspondent? (Affects access and potential capture)
3. Is this institutional communication or independent journalism?
4. What national perspective might affect coverage?

**EU institution sources**:
- European Commission communications serve Commission interests
- European Parliament communications may vary by political group
- EU agencies have institutional perspectives
- Treat as primary sources about their own positions, not neutral analysis

## Expert Perspective: Research on Bias Recognition

### Key Findings

**The bias blind spot (Pronin et al., 2002)**:

People are better at detecting bias in others than in themselves. When evaluating others' judgments, we see bias; when evaluating our own, we see objectivity.

*Implication*: Build systematic checks that don't rely on subjective self-assessment. Ask: "Would I accept this from a source with opposite perspective?"

**Motivated reasoning and bias perception (Kahan et al., various)**:

People's perception of bias in others correlates with how much they disagree with the conclusion. The same source is rated as more "biased" by those who disagree with its conclusions.

*Implication*: "This is biased" often means "I disagree." Separate bias assessment from agreement assessment.

**The hostile media effect (Vallone et al., 1985)**:

Partisans on both sides perceive neutral coverage as biased against them. The same news coverage appears biased in opposite directions to opposing partisans.

*Implication*: Perception of media bias is partly about perceiver, not just media. Claims that media is "biased against us" may reflect partisan perception, not objective bias.

### Practitioner Insights

**From journalists**:
- "Everyone accuses us of bias, from opposite directions"
- "Perfect objectivity is impossible; transparency about perspective is possible"
- "My bias blind spots are where my assumptions feel like facts"

**From fact-checkers**:
- "We check sources we agree with more carefully because that's where we might be lenient"
- "Bias claims often substitute for substantive disagreement"
- "The question isn't 'is it biased' but 'is it accurate'"

**From researchers**:
- "Funding disclosure helps readers assess, but doesn't automatically indicate bias"
- "Ideological diversity in research teams improves quality"
- "Replication is the ultimate bias check"

### Open Questions

**Can AI help detect bias?**
Tools exist to rate media bias, but they embed their own assumptions. AI might help with consistency but can't substitute for understanding context.

**Is bias increasing?**
Debated. More visible due to social media, but unclear if actual bias has increased or awareness has.

**Can we design bias-resistant information systems?**
"Debiasing" interventions show limited effectiveness. Exposure to diverse sources may help more than bias labels.

## Historical Context: The Evolution of Media Bias

### The "Objective Journalism" Era

**Origins (late 19th-early 20th century)**:
- Professionalization of journalism
- Separation of news and opinion
- "Objectivity" as journalistic norm
- Wire services serving diverse clients needed to be non-partisan

**What "objectivity" meant**:
- Not absence of perspective, but adherence to norms
- Verification, fairness to multiple sides, separation of fact and opinion
- Professional standards, not personal beliefs

**Limitations recognized even then**:
- Selection of what to cover reflects values
- Framing is inherent to storytelling
- "Objectivity" norm was itself culturally specific (Anglo-American)

### The Partisan Press Tradition

**European context**:
- Many European countries have/had partisan press traditions
- Newspapers openly aligned with parties or movements
- Readers knew the bias and could compensate
- Transparency about perspective rather than claimed neutrality

**Advantages**:
- No pretence of objectivity
- Readers chose sources aligned with their values
- Opinion clearly marked

**Disadvantages**:
- Filter bubbles before the internet
- Readers might never encounter opposing views
- Facts could be subordinated to partisan narrative

### Current Fragmentation

**Digital disruption effects**:
- Gatekeepers (editors, producers) lost power
- Direct publishing bypasses traditional media
- Algorithm-driven content selection
- Economic pressure on journalism

**New bias concerns**:
- Algorithmic bias (what gets amplified)
- Outrage economics (engagement over accuracy)
- Native advertising (paid content disguised as journalism)
- Influencer bias (undisclosed sponsorships)

**Future trajectory**:
- Some outlets return to explicit perspective (Substack model)
- Others emphasise transparency over neutrality claims
- Readers increasingly need to assess bias themselves
- Media literacy becomes essential skill

## Quick Reference: Bias Assessment Checklist

### Rapid Assessment (5 minutes)

- [ ] What's this source's known perspective?
- [ ] Does the claim serve or contradict that perspective?
- [ ] Can I verify the factual claims independently?
- [ ] Am I applying the same scrutiny to sources I agree with?

### Thorough Assessment (15 minutes)

All of the above, plus:
- [ ] What type of bias is operating? (Ideological, financial, selection, framing)
- [ ] What's the funding/ownership structure?
- [ ] What's omitted that other sources include?
- [ ] Does this source have a track record of accuracy?
- [ ] What would an opposing source say?

### Self-Check for Symmetric Standards

Before concluding a source is biased:
- [ ] Would I call a source with opposite perspective "biased" for similar practices?
- [ ] Am I dismissing this because it's biased or because I disagree?
- [ ] What source without this bias makes a different factual claim?
- [ ] Can I identify my own bias on this topic?

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (bias affecting reliability):
- Source has direct financial interest in the claim
- Track record of errors in predictable direction
- Claim contradicted by sources without this bias
- Source violates own standards for this claim

**Medium signals** (bias present, impact unclear):
- Known ideological perspective relevant to topic
- Selective coverage pattern
- Framing clearly favours particular conclusion

**Weak signals** (insufficient for bias diagnosis):
- You disagree with the conclusion (disagreement ≠ bias)
- Source has a perspective (all sources do)
- Source is labeled "biased" by opponents

**Against-interest exception**: When a source reports something contrary to their known bias, confidence in that specific claim increases.

---

## Stop Rule

Stop bias analysis when:
- **Factual claim is verifiable**: Check the fact, not the source bias
- **Bias doesn't affect this claim**: Known bias is irrelevant to specific claim
- **You're avoiding engagement**: "It's biased" shouldn't be a thought-terminating cliché
- **Your own bias is the issue**: Double-check your symmetrical application of standards
- **Symmetric check complete**: You've applied same scrutiny to sources on all sides

---

## DIM Mapping

Bias awareness informs intervention choice:

**Gen 2** (Debunking): Be transparent about your own potential biases in corrections; don't assume opposing source is biased while yours isn't
**Gen 3** (Prebunking): Teach bias recognition as a skill (including self-recognition); inoculate against dismissing information via "biased" label
**Gen 4** (Moderation): Bias alone shouldn't drive content decisions—reliability and accuracy matter more; don't mistake bias for misinformation
**Gen 5** (Interaction): Design for diverse source exposure, not bias labels; reduce filter bubbles that prevent encountering different perspectives

---

## Key Takeaway

Bias is universal but not uniform. The question isn't "is this source biased?" (they all are) but "does this bias affect reliability for this specific claim?" Apply your standards symmetrically—check sources you agree with as carefully as those you don't. "Biased" is often used to dismiss inconvenient information without engagement. Be specific about what type of bias, how it affects the claim, and whether you'd apply the same scrutiny to a source with opposite bias.

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: An article from an advocacy organisation reports a statistic that supports their cause. The statistic comes from government data. Someone dismisses the article as "biased."

**Question**: How do you assess whether the bias matters here?

### Your answer should include:

1. **What type of bias** is present
2. **Whether it affects** the reliability of this specific claim
3. **What you'd verify** independent of the source
4. **Self-check**: Would you evaluate a counter-advocacy source the same way?

### Extended test: The funding disclosure

A researcher discloses industry funding for a study. The study's conclusions favour the industry. Another researcher with no disclosed funding reaches opposite conclusions.

**Questions**:
1. Does funding disclosure automatically invalidate the first study?
2. What would you check before deciding which to trust?
3. What if the unfunded researcher has ideological commitments to the opposite conclusion?

**Penalty for**: Dismissing sources based on label rather than analysis, asymmetric bias scrutiny, using "biased" as thought-terminating cliché
**Reward for**: Distinguishing bias types, assessing relevance to specific claims, symmetric standards, recognising own potential bias
