## Purpose

You'll learn to assess source reliability efficiently—not to become a professional fact-checker, but to make better decisions about what information deserves your trust.

**Output format**: Assessment → Confidence (low/med/high) → Next action

---

## Scenario

An article supports a claim you're investigating. Before citing it, you need to assess the source. You have:

- **Time available**: 5-10 minutes per source (proportional to stakes)
- **Stakes**: Your credibility depends on source quality
- **Data access**: The article, basic web search capability
- **Constraint**: You can't verify everything deeply—need efficient heuristics

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: How reliable is this source for this claim?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High
3. **Next action**: Cite, seek additional sources, or discard?

---

## Method: Source Evaluation Workflow

### Start here: Lateral reading

Don't start by reading the source itself deeply. Instead:
- Open new tabs and search for information ABOUT the source
- What do others say about this publication/author?
- Is this source known for accuracy in this domain?

**Strong signal**: Established reputation, cited by other credible sources
**Medium signal**: Unknown but professional presentation
**Weak signal**: Self-claims of credibility ("trusted source")

### Step 2: Check author/publication credentials

- Who wrote this? What's their expertise in THIS topic?
- What's the publication's track record?
- Is there editorial oversight or is this self-published?

**Domain match matters**: A physics PhD isn't automatically credible on epidemiology.

### Step 3: Assess evidence quality

- Does the article cite primary sources?
- Can you verify key claims independently?
- Are claims proportionate to evidence presented?

---

## Source Hierarchy (General Guide)

**Higher reliability** (for factual claims):
- Peer-reviewed academic sources (in their domain)
- Established news organisations with editorial standards
- Official statistics from methodologically transparent bodies
- Primary sources you can verify

**Medium reliability**:
- Expert commentary (check domain match)
- Reputable trade/specialist publications
- Government sources (check for political framing)

**Lower reliability** (require corroboration):
- Advocacy organisations (even credible ones have agendas)
- Individual social media accounts
- Anonymous or unclear sourcing
- Sources that primarily aggregate others' work

**Context matters**: A source unreliable for health claims might be reliable for political opinion.

## Case Study 1: The Lancet Hydroxychloroquine Retraction (2020)

### Background

In May 2020, The Lancet—one of the world's most prestigious medical journals—published a study claiming hydroxychloroquine increased mortality in COVID-19 patients. The study influenced WHO to pause clinical trials. Within weeks, it was retracted due to data integrity concerns.

### Timeline

**May 22, 2020**: Study published in The Lancet, based on data from Surgisphere, a small analytics company.

**May 25-28, 2020**: Independent researchers raise concerns. The dataset claimed to include patient data from hundreds of hospitals across six continents. Researchers in Australia and Africa noted their countries' data didn't match known hospital records.

**June 2, 2020**: The Lancet issues "Expression of Concern." Multiple researchers request data access for verification.

**June 4, 2020**: Surgisphere refuses independent audit, citing confidentiality. Three of four authors request retraction.

**June 4, 2020**: The Lancet retracts the study.

### Analysis: Source Evaluation Lessons

**What made this source appear reliable (high confidence signals that proved misleading)**:

- Published in The Lancet (among the highest-reputation medical journals)
- Large dataset (96,032 patients from 671 hospitals)
- Multiple named authors with academic affiliations
- Rapid peer review during pandemic urgency

**What should have raised questions (medium signals that were underweighted)**:

- Surgisphere was a small, relatively unknown company claiming to have database access that major institutions lacked
- The lead author had no prior publications using this dataset
- Data granularity claimed was implausible given known data-sharing barriers
- No independent verification of the underlying data before publication

**Key lesson**: Prestige of publication is a useful heuristic but not sufficient. The Lancet's peer review relied on data it couldn't verify. Lateral reading about Surgisphere—rather than about The Lancet—would have revealed a company with minimal public track record claiming extraordinary data access.

### What This Means for Your Practice

When evaluating sources with prestigious publication venues:

1. **Publication prestige isn't data verification**: Journals verify methodology, not raw data authenticity
2. **Check the data source, not just the analysis**: Who collected the data? Can it be independently verified?
3. **Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence**: A dataset larger than any existing database should trigger questions
4. **Speed creates vulnerabilities**: Pandemic-era rapid publication increased error rates across all journals

**Confidence calibration**: Even sources from top-tier venues warrant medium confidence until independently corroborated. This isn't cynicism—it's appropriate epistemic humility.

## Case Study 2: The "Swedish Expert" COVID Disinformation Network (2020-2021)

### Background

During 2020-2021, a network of websites and social media accounts promoted COVID-19 misinformation attributed to Swedish doctors and scientists. Investigation revealed many "experts" either didn't exist, had credentials misrepresented, or had their statements fabricated or taken out of context.

### The Source Evaluation Challenge

Content appeared on professional-looking websites with:
- Doctor names and photos
- Claimed affiliations with Swedish institutions
- Medical terminology and citations
- Multilingual versions suggesting established infrastructure

### Investigation Process

Researchers applied lateral reading:

**Step 1**: Search for the named doctors outside the promoting websites
- Some names returned no results in Swedish medical registries
- Others were real doctors whose quotes were fabricated or distorted
- Some were real people but with no medical credentials

**Step 2**: Check the websites themselves
- WHOIS lookups showed recent registration, often through privacy services
- No physical address or verifiable contact information
- Cross-linked with known disinformation networks

**Step 3**: Verify specific claims
- Claimed institutional affiliations didn't check out
- "Studies" cited either didn't exist or said something different

### Key Lessons

**Professional appearance is cheap**: Website design, photos, and technical language cost little to produce. These are weak signals of reliability.

**Lateral reading would have caught this quickly**: 5 minutes searching for the "experts" outside the promoting sites revealed the problems. Reading the content itself more carefully would not have helped—the deception was in the source identity, not internal inconsistencies.

**Verify credentials at the source**: If someone claims to be a doctor at Karolinska Institute, check Karolinska's directory—not Google results that might include the disinformation itself.

## Case Study 3: Wikipedia Citation Chains (Ongoing Pattern)

### Background

Wikipedia is often dismissed as unreliable, but the reality is more nuanced. Wikipedia itself isn't a primary source—it's a tertiary source that aggregates and cites others. The reliability question is about the underlying citation chain.

### The Pattern

Researchers investigating specific claims have documented circular citation patterns:

1. False or unsourced claim appears on Wikipedia
2. News article cites Wikipedia (sometimes without attribution)
3. Wikipedia article is updated to cite the news article as "source"
4. The false claim now appears "sourced" but the chain is circular

### Example: The "Citogenesis" Problem

The term "citogenesis" was coined to describe this phenomenon after an xkcd comic. Real examples include:

- A comedian's joke birthdate was added to Wikipedia, then cited by media, then cited back
- Fake historical "facts" inserted by vandals that persisted because they acquired citations
- Self-published claims laundered into apparent credibility through the chain

### What This Means for Source Evaluation

**Follow citations to their origin**: Don't stop at "this is sourced." Check whether the source actually supports the claim and whether it's independent.

**Distinguish citation types**:
- Primary source: Original data, direct observation, official document
- Secondary source: Analysis or reporting on primary sources
- Tertiary source: Aggregation of secondary sources (encyclopedias, textbooks)

**Wikipedia is a starting point, not an endpoint**: Its value is the citation links. Follow them.

**Recency of Wikipedia edits matters**: Recently-edited articles on contentious topics may be less stable than long-established consensus articles.

## Tool Tutorial: Lateral Reading in Practice

### What Is Lateral Reading?

Lateral reading means leaving the source to verify it, rather than reading it more closely. Professional fact-checkers spend most of their time reading ABOUT sources rather than reading the sources themselves.

### Step-by-Step Process

**Step 1: Open new tabs**

Before reading the article closely, open a search engine in a new tab.

**Step 2: Search for the source**

Search for: [publication name] + "reliability" or "bias" or "credibility"

Also try: [publication name] + "criticism" or "controversy"

**What you're looking for**:
- Wikipedia article about the publication (check the "Reception" or "Criticism" sections)
- Media Bias/Fact Check or similar rating services (use as one input, not definitive)
- Academic or journalistic analysis of the outlet
- Whether the outlet appears in fact-check databases as a repeat source of false claims

**Step 3: Search for the author**

Search for: [author name] + [topic]

**What you're looking for**:
- Other work by this author
- Their credentials and whether they match the topic
- Whether they're cited by credible sources in this domain
- Any controversies or retractions

**Step 4: Check the specific claims**

For key factual claims in the article:
- Search for the claim independently
- Find the primary source if one is cited
- Check whether other credible sources report the same thing

### Tool: WHOIS Lookup for Website Age

For unfamiliar websites, check when they were registered:

1. Go to whois.domaintools.com or similar service
2. Enter the domain
3. Check "Creation Date"

**Interpretation**:
- Site claiming long history but registered recently: Medium signal of concern
- Site registered through privacy service: Common for legitimate sites, but combined with other signals may indicate intent to obscure
- This is one data point, not definitive—many legitimate sites are new

### Tool: Wayback Machine for History

Check how a source has changed over time:

1. Go to web.archive.org
2. Enter the URL
3. Browse historical snapshots

**What to look for**:
- Has the site's claimed identity or mission changed dramatically?
- Were claims that are now presented as established previously presented as speculation?
- Has content been quietly edited without correction notices?

### Limitations

These tools help but aren't definitive:
- WHOIS data can be masked or transferred
- Wayback Machine doesn't capture everything
- Absence of negative information isn't proof of reliability
- These checks take time—proportion to stakes

## Practice Exercise 1: The Health News Article

### Scenario

You're researching vaccine hesitancy for a report. You find an article titled "New Study Questions Vaccine Schedule Safety" on a website called HealthNewsDaily.com. The article cites "researchers at Stanford" and includes statistics.

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. Describe your lateral reading process
2. What would you search for first?
3. What signals would make you trust vs. distrust this source?
4. At what point would you stop and either use or discard the source?

### Constraints

- You cannot contact Stanford directly
- You have only web search access
- The stakes are medium (internal report, not public statement)

### Worked Solution

**First search**: "HealthNewsDaily.com" + reliability

**Possible findings and interpretation**:

*If found*: Media Bias/Fact Check rates it as "mixed" factual reporting with a "natural health" orientation
- Interpretation: Medium signal of concern for medical claims; would need corroboration

*If not found*: No ratings or reputation information; site is unknown
- Interpretation: Weak signal either way; proceed to other checks

**Second search**: Check the Stanford claim

Search: "Stanford" + [specific claim from headline/article]

**Possible findings**:

*If found*: Stanford press release or faculty page confirms the research
- Interpretation: Strong signal; the claim about Stanford researchers is accurate
- Next step: Read the actual study, not just the HealthNewsDaily interpretation

*If not found*: No Stanford confirmation of this research
- Interpretation: Medium-to-strong signal of concern; the Stanford attribution may be false or exaggerated
- Next step: Search for the specific researcher names if provided

**Third check**: WHOIS for domain

**Possible findings**:

*Recent registration (last 6 months) + privacy protection*: Combined with other signals, suggests caution
*Established domain (5+ years)*: Not definitive but one positive signal

**Decision framework**:

| Scenario | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Stanford confirms, site has mixed reputation | Go to primary source (the study); cite study, not HealthNewsDaily |
| Stanford doesn't confirm, site unknown | Discard; seek alternative sources |
| Stanford confirms, site has good reputation | Can cite with confidence, but still prefer primary source |
| Claims match other credible sources | Can use as one of multiple sources |

**Stop rule**: Stop when you've either (a) found the primary source and can cite that instead, or (b) found sufficient reason to discard, or (c) 15 minutes elapsed for this medium-stakes task.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Reading the article more carefully instead of reading about the source**: Internal consistency doesn't prove accuracy
2. **Trusting the Stanford reference without checking**: Institutional names are often dropped without authorization
3. **Spending an hour on a medium-stakes source**: Proportionality matters
4. **Discarding immediately because of unfamiliar site name**: Unknown isn't the same as unreliable

## Practice Exercise 2: The Viral Social Media Claim

### Scenario

A tweet with 50,000 retweets claims: "EXPOSED: EU document proves Brussels planned pandemic response before COVID existed. Link in thread."

The linked document is a PDF on what appears to be an EU website. The tweet author has "Dr." in their display name and 200,000 followers.

**Your task (10 minutes)**:

1. Evaluate the source (the Twitter account)
2. Evaluate the evidence (the PDF)
3. What's your assessment and confidence?
4. What would you do next?

### Worked Solution

**Evaluating the Twitter account**:

Search: [Username] + credentials OR [Display name "Dr."] + verification

**Signals to check**:
- Is there independent verification of the doctorate claim?
- What field is the doctorate in? (Domain match for EU policy analysis?)
- What's the account's history? (Lateral reading about the account)
- Follower count is NOT a reliability signal

**Evaluating the PDF**:

**Step 1**: Verify it's actually on an EU domain
- Check the URL carefully (not eu-documents.com but actual europa.eu)
- If hosted elsewhere, immediate medium signal of concern

**Step 2**: Find the document through official channels
- Go to europa.eu and search for the document title/number
- If it exists, read the actual EU version, not the linked one (PDFs can be modified)

**Step 3**: Read what the document actually says
- "Pandemic preparedness" documents exist from before COVID (this is normal—pandemic planning is standard)
- "Planned response" ≠ "planned pandemic"
- The framing may be misleading even if the document is real

**Assessment**:

The pattern here is common: real document + misleading interpretation + credentials display + viral spread.

**Likely assessment**: Low confidence in the claim as presented. The document may be real but the interpretation ("proves Brussels planned pandemic") is almost certainly wrong based on how pandemic preparedness documents work.

**Next action**: If stakes warrant, verify document authenticity and read it yourself. The viral claim is not usable as-is.

### Common Mistakes

1. **Trusting the "Dr." title**: Display names aren't verified; even if real, domain expertise matters
2. **Assuming high engagement means credibility**: Viral spread correlates with emotional resonance, not accuracy
3. **Trusting the PDF because it looks official**: Verify through official channels
4. **Dismissing because it's Twitter**: The claim might lead to a real document worth examining—evaluate the evidence, not the platform

## Practice Exercise 3: The Expert Commentary

### Scenario

You're writing about AI regulation. You find an op-ed in a business publication by a "former tech executive and AI ethics researcher" arguing against regulation. The piece makes several factual claims about AI capabilities and regulatory impacts in the EU.

**Your task (12 minutes)**:

1. How do you evaluate the author's credentials?
2. How do you handle the factual claims?
3. How do you account for potential conflicts of interest?
4. When is this source usable, and how?

### Worked Solution

**Credential evaluation**:

Search: [Author name] + LinkedIn / professional history

**Check**:
- Which tech company? What role? (Executive at Google ≠ executive at a 5-person startup)
- "AI ethics researcher" where? Published where? Cited by whom?
- Is this someone whose views are taken seriously by other experts in the field?

**Factual claims**:

Op-eds mix opinion (acceptable to attribute) with factual claims (need verification).

**Separate and verify**:
- Opinion: "AI regulation would harm innovation" → Attribute as this author's view
- Factual claim: "EU AI Act would ban X" → Verify independently against the actual regulation
- Factual claim: "AI can/cannot do Y" → Check against technical sources

**Conflict of interest assessment**:

Search: [Author name] + current affiliations / advisory roles / investments

**Relevant questions**:
- Does the author have financial interest in the regulatory outcome?
- Are they paid by entities affected by regulation?
- Conflicts don't disqualify, but they inform interpretation

**Usability assessment**:

| Scenario | Use |
|----------|-----|
| Credentialed expert with disclosed conflicts | Usable for perspective; verify factual claims independently; note potential conflict |
| Credentials don't check out | Not usable as expert source |
| Factual claims check out independently | Citable for those specific facts |
| Opinion piece from known advocate | Usable as "advocates argue..." with attribution |

**Stop rule**: For medium-stakes use, 12 minutes is proportionate. Verify the most important factual claim; assess credentials enough to decide on attribution framing; note any obvious conflicts.

## Regional Focus: Source Evaluation in the EU Context

### The Multilingual Challenge

EU information environments span 24 official languages and dozens of national media landscapes. This creates specific source evaluation challenges:

**Translation-based manipulation**: Content can be translated with subtle changes that alter meaning. A statement "accurate" in German may be misleading when rendered in English.

**Cross-border reputation opacity**: A highly credible outlet in Finland may be unknown to an analyst in Portugal, and vice versa. National reputations don't travel well.

**EU institution complexity**: Legitimate EU documents exist across multiple institutions (Commission, Parliament, Council, agencies) with different publication practices. Knowing where to verify requires institutional knowledge.

### EU-Specific Source Categories

**EU institutions** (primary sources):
- europa.eu is the central portal
- Each institution has its own site (ec.europa.eu, europarl.europa.eu, etc.)
- EUR-Lex for legal documents
- Verify through official channels, not PDFs from other sites

**National media landscape considerations**:
- Public broadcasters vary in independence and quality by country
- Some countries have stronger press freedom traditions than others
- Media ownership concentration differs significantly
- Check Press Freedom Index and national media analysis

**EU-focused disinformation sources**:
- EUvsDisinfo maintains a database of pro-Kremlin disinformation, but note it's itself an advocacy source (use for leads, verify independently)
- Some outlets present themselves as EU-critical journalism but are actually coordinated disinformation operations
- Anti-EU framing isn't automatically disinformation, but some outlets mix legitimate criticism with false claims

### Language-Specific Source Evaluation

**For sources in languages you don't read**:
- Machine translation for initial assessment, but be cautious about nuance
- Seek native-speaker verification for high-stakes use
- Check whether the source is cited by credible outlets in that language

**For translated sources**:
- Try to find the original if possible
- Note when you're relying on translation
- Be aware that quotes are particularly vulnerable to translation manipulation

### EU Regulatory Context for Platforms

The Digital Services Act creates new source-labeling requirements:
- Very large platforms must take measures against illegal content
- Disinformation codes create some transparency obligations
- These don't guarantee accuracy but create accountability mechanisms

This regulatory context means platforms operating in the EU may have different policies than global defaults—check for EU-specific labels and warnings.

## Expert Perspective: Research on Source Evaluation

### Key Findings from Research

**Lateral reading effectiveness (Wineburg et al., 2019)**:

Professional fact-checkers spent more time reading ABOUT sources (lateral reading) than reading the sources themselves. Students and academics spent more time reading the source deeply (vertical reading). Fact-checkers were more efficient and accurate.

*Implication*: Teach lateral reading, not just critical reading.

**Confirmation bias in source evaluation (various studies)**:

People evaluate sources that confirm their beliefs less critically than sources that challenge them. This bias affects experts and novices, though in different ways.

*Implication*: Build explicit checks into your workflow that don't depend on your reaction to the content.

**Prestige heuristic limitations (Metzger et al., 2010)**:

People use shortcuts like website design, institution names, and professional appearance to judge credibility. These correlate weakly with actual accuracy.

*Implication*: Professional appearance is easy to fake; lateral reading catches what design evaluation misses.

### Practitioner Insights

**From professional fact-checkers**:
- "I spend 80% of my time on the source and 20% on the content"
- "The most dangerous sources look professional and cite real institutions"
- "When something confirms what I want to believe, I know I need to check harder"

**From investigative journalists**:
- "Follow the document to its origin, not to the place you found it"
- "Anyone claiming exclusive access to information should explain how they got it"
- "If I can't verify the source, I can't use the information—no matter how interesting"

### Open Questions

**How much verification is enough?**

There's no universal answer. Proportionality frameworks suggest matching verification effort to stakes, but this is judgment-dependent.

**How do we handle sources we can't read?**

Machine translation helps but introduces errors. The field lacks good protocols for cross-language verification at scale.

**How do we train source evaluation efficiently?**

Research suggests lateral reading can be taught, but habit formation takes practice. Single interventions have limited lasting effect.

## Historical Context: Source Reliability Over Time

### Pre-Digital Era

Source evaluation has always mattered, but the landscape was different:

**Gatekeeping provided default filtering**: Fewer sources reached mass audiences; editorial and publishing barriers created some quality floor (not perfect, but different from today)

**Verification was slower but more stable**: Without real-time updates, sources couldn't be modified after publication; what you cited stayed fixed

**Expertise was harder to fake**: Claiming credentials required more than a display name; verification through professional networks was slower but more robust

### Digital Transition

The internet changed the source landscape:

**Publication barriers dropped**: Anyone can publish, reducing reliance on gatekeepers but also removing their filtering function

**Speed increased, verification time compressed**: Claims spread faster than they can be checked; source evaluation competes with attention cycles

**Remix culture complicates attribution**: Content is quoted, screenshotted, edited, and shared in ways that obscure origins

### Current State

Today's source evaluation challenges include:

**Synthetic content**: AI-generated text and images can create sources that never existed

**Coordinated inauthenticity**: Networks of fake sources can create false consensus and circular citation

**Platform-mediated trust**: Social signals (likes, shares) have become proxy credibility markers despite weak correlation with accuracy

**Information overload**: More sources than anyone can evaluate creates dependence on heuristics that can be gamed

### Future Considerations

Emerging challenges for source evaluation:

**AI-generated "experts"**: Fake personas with fabricated credentials and publication records

**Real-time deep fakes**: Video and audio of real people saying things they didn't say

**Automated source laundering**: Using AI to create citation chains that appear independent

**Provenance technology**: Potential solutions through content authenticity initiatives, but adoption is slow and imperfect

## Quick Reference: Source Evaluation Checklist

### 5-Minute Check (Low Stakes)

- [ ] Lateral search: What do others say about this source?
- [ ] Author credentials: Do they have relevant expertise?
- [ ] Other coverage: Do credible sources report the same thing?
- [ ] Basic plausibility: Are claims proportionate to evidence?

### 15-Minute Check (Medium Stakes)

All of the above, plus:
- [ ] Primary source: Can you find and verify the underlying evidence?
- [ ] Publication history: Does this outlet have a track record in this domain?
- [ ] Conflict check: Does the author have relevant financial or ideological interests?
- [ ] Domain match: Is the source credible for THIS specific type of claim?

### Deep Verification (High Stakes)

All of the above, plus:
- [ ] Independent corroboration: Multiple independent sources confirm
- [ ] Expert consultation: Domain expert has reviewed the claims
- [ ] Document verification: Original documents obtained through official channels
- [ ] Historical consistency: Source has track record of accuracy over time

### Decision Framework

| Signals | Action |
|---------|--------|
| Strong positive signals, corroborated | Cite with confidence |
| Mixed signals | Cite with caveats, seek additional sources |
| Unknown source, unverifiable | Do not cite; seek alternative |
| Clear negative signals | Discard; document for future reference |

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (reliable source):
- Consistent track record verified by independent assessment
- Transparent methodology and sourcing
- Corrections policy and demonstrated willingness to correct errors
- Cited by other credible sources in the domain

**Medium signals** (use with caution):
- Professional presentation but limited track record
- Credible in some domains but not this one
- Possible conflicts of interest acknowledged

**Weak signals** (insufficient for reliability):
- Source claims to be reliable (self-assessment)
- Source agrees with your priors (confirmation trap)
- Source looks professional (appearance isn't reliability)
- Source has many followers (popularity isn't accuracy)

---

## Stop Rule

Stop source evaluation when:
- **Reliability established**: Source passes checks, stakes don't require more
- **Reliability disqualified**: Clear problems found, move to alternatives
- **Time disproportionate**: For low-stakes claims, quick checks suffice
- **Primary source available**: Go to the primary source instead

## Key Takeaway

Source evaluation is about proportionate effort. You can't deeply verify everything, so develop efficient heuristics: lateral reading first, domain-specific credibility check, evidence quality assessment. The goal isn't perfect certainty—it's making better decisions faster about what deserves trust. Prestige and appearance are weak signals; independent verification and track record are strong signals.
