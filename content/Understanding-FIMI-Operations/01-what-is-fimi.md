## Purpose

You'll learn to identify coordinated information operations by assessing harm, intent, and coordination patterns—not by hunting for "foreignness" as proof.

**Output format**: Assessment → Confidence (low/med/high) → Next action

---

## Scenario

Your media monitoring team notices coordinated amplification of corruption allegations against government officials. The campaign involves 200+ accounts, some with Russian-language interface artifacts, spreading content across three platforms over 10 days. Your director asks: "Is this FIMI?"

You have:

- **Time available**: 4 hours for initial assessment
- **Stakes**: Briefing to senior officials who will decide whether to activate foreign interference protocols
- **Data access**: Public social media data, basic account metadata, no platform cooperation yet
- **Constraint**: Claiming "FIMI" without evidence damages credibility; missing real interference has security implications

---

## Task

Produce three outputs:

1. **Assessment**: What signals suggest this is coordinated information manipulation (vs organic activism or domestic political campaigning)?
2. **Confidence level**: Low / Medium / High that foreign actors are involved
3. **Next action**: Escalate as FIMI, continue monitoring, or close investigation?

## Method: FIMI Detection Workflow

### Start here: Assess harm and intent, not origin

Before investigating foreign links:

**What harm is occurring or intended?**
- Undermining democratic processes (elections, referenda)?
- Inciting violence or targeting vulnerable groups?
- Eroding institutional trust through coordinated deception?
- Creating public health risks?

**If harm is unclear or minimal → document and stop.** Not everything coordinated is dangerous. Not everything foreign is interference.

**Strong signal**: Clear harm to democratic processes or public safety
**Weak signal**: "This feels manipulative" or "I disagree with the message"

### Step 2: Check for coordination patterns

Look for:
- **Synchronized timing**: Accounts posting identical/near-identical content within minutes
- **Network structure**: Accounts that only interact with each other, not broader community
- **Template messaging**: Variations on a central script
- **Cross-platform coordination**: Same accounts, same messages on multiple platforms
- **Amplification chains**: Content passing through networks in organized sequence

**Strong signal combo**: Synchronized timing + template messaging + cross-platform presence + isolated network structure
**Medium signal**: Some coordination indicators (activists also coordinate)
**Weak signal**: "Lots of people saying similar things" (social movements have shared language)

### Step 3: Look for deception indicators

Examine:
- **Fake personas**: Stock photos, AI-generated faces, stolen identities
- **Misleading attribution**: Accounts claiming to be local but showing foreign indicators
- **Aged accounts suddenly activated**: Long dormant, then suddenly active on this topic
- **Platform manipulation**: Fake engagement, purchased followers

**Strong signal**: Multiple accounts with fake personas + misleading claims about identity
**Medium signal**: Some suspicious accounts (some real people use stock photos)
**Weak signal**: "New accounts" (people do create accounts for activism)

### Step 4: Assess foreign attribution signals

Only after establishing harm + coordination + deception, check:
- **Language artifacts**: Translation errors, non-native grammar patterns
- **Timezone activity**: Posting patterns matching foreign locations, not claimed location
- **Infrastructure links**: IP addresses, hosting providers, payment methods
- **Content alignment**: Messaging consistently serves foreign state interests, never criticizes them

**Critical point**: Foreign origin matters for response strategy (diplomatic vs domestic), but harm + coordination + deception matter more than passport.

**Strong signal**: Multiple technical indicators + consistent foreign state interest alignment
**Medium signal**: Some foreign indicators but plausible alternative explanations
**Weak signal**: "Bad grammar" (humans make errors) or "pro-Russian content" (legitimate political positions exist)

## What FIMI Is (And Isn't)

### The EU Definition

The European External Action Service (EEAS) defines FIMI as:
> "A pattern of behaviour that threatens or has the potential to negatively impact values, procedures and political processes. Such activity is manipulative in character, conducted in an intentional and coordinated manner, by state or non-state actors, including their proxies inside and outside of their own territory."

**Key elements**:
1. **Pattern of behaviour** (not single incidents)
2. **Threatens values/processes** (harm-focused)
3. **Manipulative** (deceptive, not just persuasive)
4. **Intentional and coordinated** (not organic spread)
5. **State or non-state actors** (includes proxies)

### What FIMI Is NOT

**Not all foreign information is FIMI**:
- Foreign news coverage (even critical) is not FIMI
- Diaspora political activity is not FIMI
- International advocacy campaigns are not FIMI
- Cultural exchange is not FIMI

**Not all manipulation is FIMI**:
- Domestic disinformation is not FIMI (harmful but different response)
- Commercial misinformation is not FIMI (advertising regulation, not foreign policy)
- Individual trolls are not FIMI (harassment policy, not security response)

**Not all coordination is FIMI**:
- Political campaigns coordinate (legitimately)
- Activist movements coordinate (legitimately)
- PR firms coordinate (may be unethical but not FIMI)

### Why Definitions Matter

Calling something FIMI triggers specific responses:
- Security apparatus involvement
- Diplomatic consequences
- Platform escalation protocols
- Public communication framing

**Overcalling FIMI**:
- Damages credibility for real cases
- Stigmatizes legitimate foreign perspectives
- Can suppress domestic dissent
- Creates diplomatic incidents without cause

**Undercalling FIMI**:
- Allows foreign operations to proceed
- Fails to activate appropriate responses
- Misses patterns that indicate state involvement

## Case Study 1: Internet Research Agency (IRA) Operations (2014-2018)

### Background

The Internet Research Agency, a Russian organization, conducted extensive information operations targeting the United States, Europe, and other regions. The 2018 Mueller investigation and subsequent platform investigations documented these operations in detail.

### What Made It FIMI

**Coordination indicators (documented)**:
- Hundreds of accounts operated from single building in St. Petersburg
- Shift-based work with quotas for posts and engagement
- Content calendars and target lists
- Cross-platform operations (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube)
- Budget of millions of dollars

**Deception indicators (documented)**:
- Fake personas claiming American identities
- Accounts posing as local activists, news organizations
- Fabricated grassroots movements
- Impersonation of real Americans in some cases

**Foreign attribution (documented)**:
- Physical location in Russia confirmed
- Funding traced to entities connected to Russian government
- Content aligned with Russian state interests
- Never criticized Russian government or policies

**Harm indicators (assessed)**:
- Targeted divisive social issues to amplify conflict
- Organized real-world events under false pretenses
- Targeted election-related content
- Reached millions of Americans

### What We Can Learn

**Detection lessons**:
- Scale was detectable (hundreds of accounts with coordinated patterns)
- Fake personas were detectable (reverse image search, inconsistent details)
- Cross-platform coordination was detectable (same content, similar timing)
- Never-criticize-Russia pattern was detectable

**Attribution lessons**:
- Full attribution required insider access and investigation
- Public-facing indicators were suggestive but not conclusive
- Platform cooperation was essential for confirmation
- Intelligence community provided key links

**Response lessons**:
- Detection was years after operations began
- Many accounts reached significant audiences before removal
- Removal didn't reverse exposure effects
- Attribution enabled diplomatic and legal responses

### Cautions for Analysts

**Don't assume every coordinated campaign is IRA-style**:
- IRA was unusually well-documented
- Most operations aren't this clear-cut
- Using IRA as template may cause over-detection
- Domestic actors can mimic these patterns

**IRA evolved**:
- Tactics changed after exposure
- Later operations used different approaches
- Don't expect current operations to look like 2016

## Case Study 2: Secondary Infektion (2014-2020)

### Background

"Secondary Infektion" was a long-running Russian influence operation identified by researchers, characterized by lower scale but higher persistence than IRA. It focused on spreading forged documents and false narratives across many platforms and languages.

### What Made It Different from IRA

**Characteristics**:
- Lower volume, more targeted
- Focus on forged documents rather than social media personas
- Spread across 300+ platforms including niche forums
- Multilingual (7+ languages)
- Long-running (6+ years documented)

**Tactics**:
- Created forged official documents
- Planted stories in obscure sites to launder into mainstream
- Used throwaway accounts rather than building personas
- Targeted specific individuals and institutions

### Detection Challenges

**Why it was harder to detect**:
- Lower volume meant less signal
- Throwaway accounts harder to link
- Spread across obscure platforms less monitored
- Forgery detection requires expertise

**What eventually revealed it**:
- Cross-platform pattern analysis
- Forgery analysis of documents
- Timing and targeting analysis
- Operational security failures

### Key Lessons

1. **Not all FIMI is high-volume**: Some operations prioritize persistence over reach
2. **Platform diversity complicates detection**: Monitoring mainstream platforms isn't enough
3. **Document forgery is a FIMI tactic**: Not just social media manipulation
4. **Years can pass before detection**: Long operations may remain undetected
5. **Attribution confidence varies**: Secondary Infektion attribution is lower confidence than IRA

## Case Study 3: False Positive—Misidentifying Domestic Activism

### Background

This composite case study draws from multiple instances where domestic political activity was incorrectly labeled as foreign interference.

### The Pattern

**What happened**:
- Coordinated political activity detected
- Coordination patterns matched "FIMI indicators"
- Rushed assessment attributed to foreign actors
- Later investigation revealed domestic origin

**Why mistakes were made**:
- Coordination = FIMI assumption (wrong: activists coordinate)
- Foreign-language content = foreign operation (wrong: diaspora exists)
- Content critical of government = foreign interference (wrong: domestic dissent is legitimate)
- Urgency overrode verification

### Specific Examples (Anonymized Pattern)

**Example A: Diaspora Political Activity**
- Coordinated campaign on domestic political issue
- Accounts showed foreign-language indicators
- Content supported foreign government positions
- Assessment: Initially flagged as potential FIMI
- Reality: Legitimate diaspora community organizing

**Example B: Activist Movement**
- Rapid coordination around protest
- Messaging appeared templated
- New accounts created for campaign
- Assessment: Flagged as suspected bot network
- Reality: Organic activist coordination with shared messaging

**Example C: Opposition Politics**
- Content critical of government
- Spread through coordinated networks
- Aligned with foreign media coverage
- Assessment: Suspected foreign amplification
- Reality: Domestic opposition party campaign

### Why False Positives Are Dangerous

**Harm to democracy**:
- Stigmatizes legitimate dissent
- Enables government to dismiss criticism as "foreign"
- Chills political speech
- Damages trust in institutions making the claims

**Harm to FIMI response capacity**:
- Discredits accurate assessments
- Creates backlash against monitoring
- Provides talking points for actual foreign actors
- Reduces public trust in future warnings

### Preventing False Positives

**Procedural safeguards**:
- Require evidence beyond coordination for FIMI claim
- Distinguish foreign *origin* from foreign *alignment*
- Allow time for investigation before public claims
- Include devil's advocate in assessment process

**Analytical standards**:
- Coordination is necessary but not sufficient
- Content alignment is weak evidence
- Domestic explanations must be ruled out
- Uncertainty should be stated explicitly

## Tool Tutorial: FIMI Assessment Framework

### Step-by-Step Assessment

**Phase 1: Harm Assessment (30 minutes)**

Questions to answer:
- What is the content/behavior doing?
- Who is affected?
- What democratic process or value is threatened?
- What is the scale of impact?

| Harm Level | Indicators | Response |
|------------|------------|----------|
| High | Electoral interference, incitement, health disinformation at scale | Priority investigation |
| Medium | Institutional trust erosion, divisive amplification | Investigation if resources allow |
| Low | Marginal reach, unclear impact | Document and monitor |

**If harm is low → stop. Not everything needs FIMI investigation.**

**Phase 2: Coordination Assessment (1 hour)**

Check for:
- Synchronized posting (within minutes)
- Network isolation (only interact with each other)
- Template content (identical or near-identical)
- Cross-platform presence
- Artificial amplification signals

**Scoring**:
- 4-5 indicators: Strong coordination signals
- 2-3 indicators: Moderate signals (activists also show some)
- 0-1 indicators: Likely organic

**Phase 3: Deception Assessment (1 hour)**

Check for:
- Fake personas (reverse image search, inconsistent details)
- Identity misrepresentation (claims don't match indicators)
- Astroturfing (fake grassroots appearance)
- Fabricated content (forged documents, manipulated media)

**Key question**: Is there deception about WHO is behind this?

Coordination without deception = may be legitimate campaign
Coordination with deception = manipulative regardless of origin

**Phase 4: Attribution Assessment (1.5 hours)**

Only if Phases 1-3 show significant signals:

Check for:
- Technical indicators (infrastructure, timezone, language)
- Operational indicators (targets, timing, persistence)
- Content indicators (state interest alignment, never-criticize pattern)
- Historical patterns (matches known actor TTPs)

**Confidence levels**:

| Confidence | Evidence Required |
|------------|-------------------|
| High | Multiple technical indicators + intelligence confirmation |
| Medium | Behavioral patterns consistent + some technical signals |
| Low | Content alignment only, or single technical indicator |

### Reporting Template

```
FIMI ASSESSMENT

Harm Assessment: [High/Medium/Low]
- Specific harm identified: [description]
- Scale of impact: [reach/affected populations]

Coordination Assessment: [Strong/Moderate/Weak]
- Indicators present: [list]
- Counter-indicators: [list]

Deception Assessment: [Present/Absent/Unclear]
- Evidence of fake personas: [yes/no with details]
- Misrepresentation indicators: [description]

Foreign Attribution Assessment: [High/Medium/Low confidence]
- Technical indicators: [list]
- Behavioral indicators: [list]
- Alternative explanations: [list]

Overall Assessment: [FIMI likely / Domestic coordination / Unclear / Not FIMI]

Recommended Action: [escalate / investigate further / monitor / close]

Caveats: [what would change this assessment]
```

## Practice Exercise 1: The Election-Related Campaign

### Scenario

Three weeks before a national election, you detect:
- 400+ accounts amplifying claims about voting fraud
- Accounts created over past 6 months (not a sudden burst)
- Mix of real-looking and suspicious accounts
- Content includes some genuine concerns and some false claims
- Hashtag trending in the country
- Some amplification from accounts with foreign-language patterns
- Similar narratives appearing on RT and Sputnik

**Your task (20 minutes)**:

1. Assess harm, coordination, deception, and attribution signals
2. What's your confidence level on FIMI?
3. What would you need to increase confidence?
4. What's the appropriate response at current evidence level?

### Worked Solution

**Harm assessment**: High
- Electoral context = high stakes
- Voting fraud claims can suppress turnout or delegitimize results
- Trending hashtag indicates significant reach

**Coordination assessment**: Moderate
- Account creation pattern suggests preparation, not organic
- Mix of real and suspicious accounts complicates picture
- Hashtag trending could be organic OR coordinated

**Deception assessment**: Unclear
- "Suspicious accounts" need closer examination
- Are there fake personas or just new accounts?
- Is content fabricated or genuine concerns amplified?

**Attribution assessment**: Low-Medium
- Foreign-language patterns present but not conclusive
- RT/Sputnik coverage doesn't prove operational link
- Same narratives could spread without coordination

**Confidence on FIMI**: Low-Medium
- Electoral timing is concerning
- Coordination signals present but not definitive
- Foreign attribution weak
- Domestic political actors also use these tactics

**To increase confidence, need**:
- Platform data on account networks
- Deeper fake persona investigation
- Infrastructure analysis
- Evidence of cross-platform coordination

**Appropriate response at current evidence**:
- Don't publicly claim FIMI (evidence too weak)
- Continue investigation with higher priority (electoral timing)
- Request platform cooperation
- Monitor for escalation
- Prepare messaging about voting fraud claims (address claims, not attribution)

## Practice Exercise 2: The Health Disinformation Campaign

### Scenario

During a disease outbreak, you observe:
- Coordinated accounts spreading anti-vaccine content
- Content includes mix of genuine concerns and false claims
- Accounts appear to be real people, not bots
- Community has genuine vaccine hesitancy (cultural/historical reasons)
- Some content translated from foreign languages
- Foreign state media has covered vaccine concerns

**Your task (15 minutes)**:

1. Is this FIMI, domestic misinformation, or legitimate debate?
2. What signals would differentiate these possibilities?
3. What response is appropriate at different confidence levels?

### Worked Solution

**Key distinction**:

This scenario mixes three possibilities:
- Genuine community concerns (legitimate)
- Domestic anti-vaccine activism (problematic but not FIMI)
- Foreign amplification of existing divisions (FIMI if coordinated/deceptive)

**Signals that would indicate domestic activity**:
- Accounts have long histories in the community
- Activity matches community organizing patterns
- No hidden coordination infrastructure
- Cultural/historical reasons for vaccine hesitancy are real

**Signals that would indicate FIMI**:
- Fake personas pretending to be community members
- Coordinated amplification from foreign infrastructure
- Content translation from foreign sources as origin (not just overlap)
- Timing aligned with foreign messaging objectives

**Signals insufficient for FIMI conclusion**:
- Foreign media covers the story (media covers real controversies)
- Content "aligns with" foreign narratives (convergent interests happen)
- Translated content exists (diaspora translates content)

**Appropriate response by confidence**:

| Confidence | Response |
|------------|----------|
| High (FIMI) | Platform action, foreign interference protocols |
| Medium | Investigate further, prepare for both scenarios |
| Low (domestic) | Public health response, not security response |

**Key insight**: Health misinformation is harmful regardless of origin. The response to the health harm should proceed even without FIMI attribution. Don't delay public health messaging waiting for attribution.

## Regional Focus: FIMI in the EU Context

### EU Institutional Framework

**Key EU bodies**:
- EEAS East StratCom Task Force (monitoring, EUvsDisinfo)
- European Commission (DSA enforcement, code of practice)
- ENISA (cyber security, related infrastructure)
- Europol (if criminal dimension)
- National security services (member state level)

**EU definition evolution**:
- FIMI terminology adopted ~2020-2021
- Emphasis on "behavior" over "content"
- Focus on coordination and deception
- Designed to avoid content-based censorship concerns

### Country-Specific Vulnerabilities

**Baltic States**:
- Russian-speaking minorities (target and concern)
- Historical tensions
- Extensive monitoring capacity
- Experience with Russian information operations

**Eastern Europe**:
- Variable monitoring capacity
- Some political polarization around East-West
- Historical and current Russian influence attempts
- EU/NATO membership debates

**Western Europe**:
- Different threat perceptions
- Focus on electoral integrity
- Platform headquarters jurisdiction
- Varied national responses

### Cross-Border Challenges

**What makes EU FIMI response difficult**:
- 27 member states with different languages
- Different legal frameworks
- Different threat perceptions
- Information flows across borders; responses stop at them

**Coordination mechanisms**:
- EDMO (European Digital Media Observatory)
- Rapid Alert System
- Strategic Communication networks
- But: Varying national commitment

## Expert Perspective: The Attribution Challenge

### Research Findings

**On attribution confidence** (various academic studies):
- Public attribution claims often exceed evidence
- Technical attribution is harder than presented
- Behavioral attribution is suggestive not conclusive
- False flags and mistakes are common

**On response effectiveness**:
- Exposure doesn't necessarily deter
- Removal is temporary (new accounts emerge)
- Public attribution can backfire (Streisand effect)
- Prebunking more effective than post-hoc response

### Practitioner Insights

**From government analysts**:
- "We often know more than we can say publicly"
- "Public confidence levels should be lower than private assessments"
- "Attribution is a political decision, not just technical"

**From platform investigators**:
- "We see coordination patterns but often can't determine origin"
- "Foreign and domestic actors use same tools and tactics"
- "Attribution confidence varies by operation"

**From researchers**:
- "Published attributions are often the clearest cases"
- "Many operations are never publicly attributed"
- "Uncertainty is the norm, not the exception"

### Open Questions

**Unresolved debates**:
- How much evidence is enough for public attribution?
- Should responses require attribution or act on behavior?
- How to handle domestic actors amplifying foreign content?
- What role for platforms vs governments in attribution?

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong signals** (coordinated information operation likely):
- Clear harm to democratic processes or public safety
- Synchronized coordination across 100+ accounts
- Fake personas with deceptive identity claims
- Cross-platform infrastructure links
- Technical indicators of foreign origin + consistent state interest alignment

**Medium signals** (worth investigating):
- Some coordination patterns
- Suspicious account characteristics
- Content amplifying divisive narratives
- Partial foreign attribution indicators

**Weak signals** (insufficient alone):
- "Content spread quickly"
- "Grammar seems off"
- "Message I disagree with"
- "Foreign language detected" (multilingual countries exist)
- "Pro-[foreign country] content" (legitimate political opinions exist)

---

## Stop Rule

Stop investigating and close assessment when:
- **Timebox reached**: 4 hours up, must report findings
- **Harm threshold not met**: Even if coordinated, impact is minimal
- **Domestic political activity**: Coordination exists but domestic actors using standard campaign tactics
- **Attribution requires platform access**: Strong coordination signals, but foreign attribution needs data you don't have

You can report "coordinated activity detected, foreign attribution unclear" without claiming FIMI. Don't overclaim.

## DIM Mapping

If you determine this is likely FIMI, which intervention fits?

**If strong signals (harm + coordination + deception + foreign attribution)**:
- **Gen 4** (Moderation): Request platform investigation under coordinated inauthentic behavior policies
- **Gen 5** (Interaction): Reduce algorithmic amplification while investigation continues
- **Diplomatic response**: If state attribution confirmed
- **Gen 2** (Debunking): Address false claims in the content

**If medium signals (coordination clear, foreign attribution unclear)**:
- **Gen 3** (Prebunking): Prepare public messaging about coordinated campaigns without claiming foreign source
- Continue monitoring
- Request platform cooperation for deeper investigation

**If weak signals (likely domestic political activity)**:
- No FIMI-specific intervention
- Standard content moderation if policies violated
- Accept this is contentious domestic politics, not foreign interference

**Key principle**: "Foreign" is not the trophy. Harm + intent + coordination + deception determine response. Foreign attribution determines which authorities respond and how.

---

## Key Takeaway

FIMI is not "information from abroad I disagree with." It's coordinated, deceptive information operations by foreign actors intended to cause harm to democratic societies. You assess: (1) Is harm occurring? (2) Is coordination present? (3) Is deception involved? (4) Are foreign actors responsible? In that order. Don't skip to "foreign" and work backwards. False positives—labeling domestic activity as FIMI—are dangerous to democracy. Appropriate confidence calibration matters as much as detection.

## Test Your Understanding

**Scenario**: 300 accounts amplify climate policy criticism during EU negotiations. Accounts have posting patterns matching US East Coast timezone. Content criticizes EU policy, praises US approach. Some accounts created recently, others aged. Messages coordinated but not identical.

**Question**: Is this FIMI? What would you check before concluding?

### Your answer should include:

1. **Hypothesis + confidence**: What type of activity is this likely to be?
2. **Three specific checks** to distinguish FIMI from legitimate US climate advocacy or domestic EU political campaigning
3. **One false positive scenario** you'll guard against (legitimate activity misidentified)
4. **Recommended next action** with minimum evidence threshold required

### Extended test: The gray zone

You detect coordination with moderate confidence. Deception is present (some fake accounts). Content is harmful. But foreign attribution is low confidence—could be domestic actors or could be foreign.

**Questions**:
1. What response is appropriate without foreign attribution?
2. What would you say publicly at this evidence level?
3. How does the response change if attribution confidence increases?

---

**Penalty for**: "US timezone = FIMI," "Criticizes EU = foreign interference," assuming coordination proves foreign origin, calling FIMI without deception evidence
**Reward for**: Harm assessment first, distinguishing coordination from deception, acknowledging legitimate foreign perspectives exist, clear evidence thresholds, appropriate confidence calibration
