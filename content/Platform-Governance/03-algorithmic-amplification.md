## Method: Amplification Analysis

### Start here: Define amplification

Algorithmic amplification: Platform systems that increase content visibility beyond what organic distribution would achieve.

**Hosting**: Making content available if someone looks for it
**Amplification**: Actively pushing content to users who didn't seek it

This distinction matters because amplification is an active choice.

### Step 2: Understand amplification mechanisms

- **Recommendation systems**: "Suggested for you"
- **Ranking algorithms**: What appears first in feeds
- **Trending systems**: What's highlighted as popular
- **Notifications**: What prompts return visits

Each represents an editorial choice, even if automated.

### Step 3: Assess amplification effects

Research suggests recommendation systems can:
- Amplify engaging content (often emotional, divisive)
- Create filter bubbles (contested extent)
- Enable rabbit holes toward extreme content
- Shape what becomes "viral"

Effects vary by platform design and content type.

---

## The Amplification Distinction

**Why it matters for policy**:

**Free speech argument for hosting**: Platforms as neutral carriers
**Weaker argument for amplification**: Active promotion is a choice

**Analogy**: 
- Library hosting a book ≠ Library featuring it in prominent display
- Both involve choice, but different levels of endorsement/responsibility

**Policy implication**: Regulating amplification is different from regulating speech.

---

## Amplification Policy Options

**Transparency requirements**:
- Disclose how algorithms rank content
- Show why content was recommended
- Research access to study effects

**User control**:
- Options for chronological feeds
- Control over recommendation inputs
- Ability to see different perspectives

**Design requirements**:
- Friction on rapid sharing
- Reduced amplification of violating content
- Circuit breakers for viral spread

**Liability approaches**:
- Responsibility for amplified (not just hosted) content
- Duty of care for recommendation systems

---

## Evidence Ladder

**Strong evidence** (amplification effect established):
- Controlled studies showing algorithmic influence on exposure
- Platform internal research confirming effects
- Consistent findings across contexts

**Medium evidence** (effect plausible):
- Observational studies suggesting relationship
- Theoretical mechanisms identified
- Some platform acknowledgment

**Weak evidence** (insufficient):
- Correlation without mechanism
- Anecdotes without systematic study
- Assumptions about how algorithms work

---

## Stop Rule

Stop amplification analysis when:
- **Mechanism unclear**: Can't specify how amplification works
- **Effects uncertain**: Evidence too weak for policy conclusions
- **Better target exists**: Content policy more effective than amplification policy

---

## DIM Mapping

Amplification is the core Gen 4-5 lever:

**Gen 4** (Moderation): Reduce amplification of violating content
**Gen 5** (Interaction): Design algorithms for information quality

**Key insight**: Moderation at amplification layer doesn't remove speech—it reduces promotion. This has different speech implications than removal.

---

## Key Takeaway

Algorithmic amplification is distinct from hosting. Platforms actively shape what billions see through recommendation systems, ranking, and trending. This is an editorial function, even if automated. Policy can target amplification without targeting speech itself—requiring transparency, user control, and responsible design.
